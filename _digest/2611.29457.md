abstract: 'Speech brain-computer interfaces aim to restore communication by translating neural activity into text, yet end-to-end decoders have historically trailed cascaded systems in accuracy. We introduce a Multi-Agent System for BraIn-to-Text (MAS-BIT), the first multi-agent framework for end-to-end brain-to-text decoding from intracortical recordings. MAS-BIT coordinates three specialized agents: a neural agent that interprets continuous neural representations, a phonetic agent that decodes soft phoneme posteriors, and an arbitration agent that integrates their complementary evidence with direct access to neural representations to generate the final transcription. The expert agents communicate with the arbiter through token-embedding-based latent communication, constructing continuous messages as probability-weighted combinations of token embeddings. These messages retain alternative token hypotheses and provide a differentiable interface through which sentence-level supervision jointly trains the neural encoders and specialized agents across participants. MAS-BIT achieves state-of-the-art end-to-end decoding performance on the Brain-to-Text ''24 and ''25 benchmarks, with word error rates of 6.929% on ''24 and 3.606% on the ''25 public test set (3.892% on the private test set). These results surpass the published BIT cascade on the ''25 public set and narrow the gap on ''24, without ensembling independently trained models or using external language-model rescoring.'

## What they do
Speech BCIs turn neural activity recorded while a person attempts to speak into text. The most accurate decoders are cascades: a neural-to-phoneme network, then n-gram/WFST search and language-model rescoring. End-to-end decoders, which feed neural features straight into a pretrained language model, have trailed them. MAS-BIT is an end-to-end decoder built from three language-model agents (a neural expert, a phonetic expert and an arbiter), trained as one model on intracortical recordings from two participants, the Brain-to-Text '24 (T12) and '25 (T15) benchmarks.

## How it works
- **Two views of one recording.** Two encoders initialized from the released BIT models produce hidden states and phoneme posteriors; participant-specific input projections let both encoders read either participant.
- **Experts and arbiter.** All agents share a frozen Qwen3-ASR-1.7B text decoder, each with its own LoRA adapter. The neural expert reads the fused hidden states, the phonetic expert reads posterior-weighted phoneme embeddings, and the arbiter reads the hidden states plus both experts' messages.
- **Latent messages.** Each expert drafts a sentence greedily, replays it, and sends at each position the probability-weighted embedding of its top 16 tokens, so the arbiter sees alternatives and its loss trains the experts and encoders.
- **Staged training** on 8,800 T12 and 8,072 T15 sentences: five-epoch stages per component, then 40 joint epochs.

## Results
- Holdout WER: 6.93% on '24 and 3.61%/3.89% on '25 public/private. Single-model BIT end-to-end gets 15.67% and 11.06%; the published BIT cascade 6.35% and 4.06% (public).
- Against a reproduced cascade on an A40 (T12): 12.7 vs 321.8 GiB host RAM, 8.6 vs 25.2 GiB GPU memory, about 1.8x faster decoding.
- Ablations: one agent given both views is 3.06 points worse on T12 (2.52/2.78 on T15); hard-text messages cost 0.23 (0.58/0.52); separate per-participant models cost 1.04 (0.52/0.74).
- When both expert drafts are wrong, the arbiter still outputs the exact sentence 31 times on T12 and 49 on T15 validation.

## Limitations
Offline evaluation on two participants. The encoders attend over whole utterances and the experts draft before the arbiter, so it does not stream yet. On '24 it remains 0.58 points behind the published cascade.
