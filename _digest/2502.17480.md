abstract: 'Modern neuroprostheses can now restore communication in patients who have lost the ability to speak or move. However, these invasive devices entail risks inherent to neurosurgery. Here, we introduce a non-invasive method to decode the production of sentences from brain activity and demonstrate its efficacy in a cohort of 35 healthy volunteers. For this, we present Brain2Qwerty, a new deep learning architecture trained to decode sentences from either electro- (EEG) or magneto-encephalography (MEG), while participants typed briefly memorized sentences on a QWERTY keyboard. With MEG, Brain2Qwerty reaches, on average, a character-error-rate (CER) of 32% and substantially outperforms EEG (CER: 67%). For the best participants, the model achieves a CER of 19%, and can perfectly decode a variety of sentences outside of the training set. While error analyses suggest that decoding depends on motor processes, the analysis of typographical errors suggests that it also involves higher-level cognitive factors. Overall, these results narrow the gap between invasive and non-invasive methods and thus open the path for developing safe brain-computer interfaces for non-communicating patients.'

## What they do
Brain-to-text neuroprostheses work well but need brain surgery. This paper asks how far non-invasive recordings can go. 35 healthy, skilled typists read a short Spanish sentence, waited, then typed it from memory on a QWERTY keyboard while EEG or MEG was recorded. Brain2Qwerty, a three-stage deep network, decodes the typed characters from those signals.

## How it works
- **Data.** Each session has 128 unique sentences of 5 to 8 words, typed with no backspace and no on-screen text. This gives 146K characters of EEG and 193K of MEG (20 participants each). MEG needed a custom keyboard with non-ferromagnetic springs.
- **Convolutional module.** A 0.5 s window of signal around each key press goes through spatial attention over sensors, a subject-specific linear layer and 8 convolutional blocks, producing one embedding per keystroke.
- **Transformer.** Four layers refine the keystroke predictions using the rest of the sentence and output logits over 29 character classes.
- **Language model.** A 9-gram character model pretrained on Spanish Wikipedia is combined with the logits in a beam search.

## Results
- Character error rate (CER) is 32% with MEG and 67% with EEG. The best and worst MEG participants reach 19% and 45%.
- Against EEGNet, Brain2Qwerty improves CER 2.25-fold on MEG and 1.14-fold on EEG. The transformer and the language model each lower CER further.
- Some held-out sentences are decoded perfectly, including ones the participant mistyped. Words absent from training are decoded poorly (68% CER).
- Decoding errors follow the keyboard: confusion rate correlates with key distance (R=0.73). CER keeps falling as training data grows.

## Limitations
The model is not real-time: it needs the full sentence and windows aligned to keystrokes. It was trained with supervision on healthy people who physically typed, so it does not apply to locked-in patients. Current MEG systems are not wearable, and a gap to invasive interfaces remains.
