abstract: 'Service robots that share corridors and lobbies with people must keep moving without endangering them. Reactive planners can stop indefinitely in dense crowds (the freezing robot problem), whereas the attention-based learned navigators that dominate the standard crowd benchmark assume holonomic motion or emit discrete actions unsuited to a differential-drive base. This paper presents TASC, a navigation system for a non-holonomic service robot that integrates an attention-based crowd encoder, an anticipatory collision feature, and a continuous-control policy. We adapt the dual-selection attention module, originally developed for discrete-action value learning, to proximal policy optimization for continuous velocity control, and add a closing-speed time-to-collision feature, a conservative estimate of the time to contact that serves as both an observation and a reward term. The policy is trained on a high-fidelity 3D robot model in NVIDIA Isaac Lab with 10,000 parallel environments, social-force-model pedestrians, and randomized crowd sizes. In 30,000 evaluation episodes, each run to its end, the policy timed out in fewer than 0.5% at every tested crowd density. This liveness comes at a cost: pedestrian collisions rise from 4.5% with five pedestrians to 17.4% with ten. In an ablation with four seeds per variant, policies retrained without the time-to-collision feature succeed 4 to 7 percentage points less often. The policy runs in Gazebo without retraining and on a commercial differential-drive service robot through a ROS 2 package with an inference latency below 10 ms, verified in a hardware-in-the-loop test with virtual pedestrians.'

## What they do
TASC is a crowd-navigation policy for MiPA, a commercial differential-drive service robot from NEURA Robotics. It takes a selective-attention crowd encoder (DSAM) that was built for value learning over 41 discrete actions, moves it to PPO with continuous, forward-only velocity commands, and adds a time-to-collision (TTC) feature. The authors present the combination and its deployment as the contribution, not the components.

## How it works
- **Observation.** Robot state plus up to 15 pedestrians, each with relative position and velocity, distance, and a closing-speed TTC normalized by a 5 s horizon. Closing-speed TTC is a lower bound on the geometric time to contact.
- **Crowd encoder.** A selective attention layer drops pedestrians scoring below mean minus one standard deviation and an inclusive layer keeps almost everyone; their outputs are averaged into a 50-D crowd vector shared by actor and critic.
- **Training.** 10,000 parallel 10 m x 10 m rooms in Isaac Lab with 0-10 social-force pedestrians that stop at random and react to the robot only within a 200° frontal arc. Nine reward terms include progress, proximity, a TTC penalty below 2.5 s and a forward-motion bonus.
- **Deployment.** A ROS 2 node runs the policy at 60 Hz with under 10 ms inference.

## Results
- Over 10,000 episodes per crowd size, success is 93.4%, 89.2% and 78.8% with 5, 7 and 10 pedestrians; timeouts stay at 0.3-0.4%.
- Pedestrian collisions rise from 4.5% to 17.4%, which the authors call the main result. With ten pedestrians, the pedestrian was approaching faster than the robot in 78% of collisions.
- Retrained without TTC (four seeds each), success is 4-7 points lower and collisions with ten pedestrians rise from 18.4% to 24.2%.
- The policy runs in Gazebo without retraining, and on the physical robot in a hardware-in-the-loop test with five virtual pedestrians.

## Limitations
All pedestrians are simulated and the robot has no pedestrian perception, so there is no sim-to-real test of navigation and nothing on social acceptability. Nothing bounds the collision rate. The scenario omits groups and narrow corridors, and the published baselines in the comparison table are not directly comparable.
