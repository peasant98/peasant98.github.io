abstract: 'Robot foundation models have shown remarkable performance in robot control, yet adapting them to a new task still relies heavily on teleoperated demonstrations. Egocentric human videos offer a scalable substitute, but overlaying rendered robots on human videos or retargeting human poses into robot actions leaves significant visual or action gaps for high-DoF robot embodiments. To address this limitation, we propose RoboTryOn, which treats human-to-robot translation as virtual try-on: try-on keeps the person and swaps the garment, RoboTryOn keeps the demonstrated motion and swaps the human for the robot. Specifically, RoboTryOn converts a human video into a video of the target robot specified by a reference image, while preserving the original task, objects, and background. To maintain visual consistency, we translate selected keyframes autoregressively and use them to guide video synthesis with motion conditioned on the human hand skeleton. Additionally, we use an inverse dynamics model (IDM) that incorporates depth features to infer action labels from synthesized videos for policy learning. Our experiments across multiple humanoid embodiments show that policies trained with our synthetic data improve the average success rate on novel tasks, including challenging bimanual manipulation, from 2% to 60%, without any real robot demonstration of those tasks. Moreover, training with our synthetic data matches the performance of policies trained solely on teleoperated demonstrations at approximately a third of the collection cost, demonstrating the cost efficiency of our approach.'

## What they do
Teaching a dexterous robot a new task usually means teleoperating it. RoboTryOn instead has a person do the task in front of a camera at the robot's head-camera position, then converts that video into a robot demonstration the way virtual try-on swaps a garment: the motion, objects and background stay, and the human arm becomes the robot. A VLA (π0.5) is fine-tuned on these synthetic episodes, alone or mixed with teleoperated ones.

## How it works
- **Keyframe translation.** Keyframes are taken where the wrists slow down. A fine-tuned FLUX.2 image model inpaints the masked human arm with the robot, guided by a retargeted 2D hand skeleton and conditioned on the previous generated keyframe so the scene stays consistent.
- **Video synthesis.** A LoRA on the LTX-2.5 video model fills in the motion between robot keyframes, following the human hand-skeleton flow.
- **Action labels.** An inverse dynamics model on a Depth Anything V2 backbone reads joint actions from the generated video; inverse kinematics then corrects the wrist position to the reconstructed human wrist.
- All three models train on robot data only (2.5-3.8 h per robot), with no paired human-robot data, and one set of models serves the OpenArm, RB-Y1 and GR-1.

## Results
- Two robots (OpenArm with 6-DoF Inspire hands, RB-Y1 with 20-DoF WujiHand2 hands), 11 tabletop tasks, 28 trials each. On the seven tasks with human videos only, success rises from 3.1% to 73.0% on the OpenArm and from 1.0% to 46.9% on the RB-Y1; EgoMimic and Masquerade stay below 10%.
- On the four pick-and-place tasks that have teleoperated data, adding the synthetic episodes raises success from 47.3% to 75.9% (OpenArm) and 69.6% to 79.5% (RB-Y1).
- Replayed open loop in simulation, the IDM labels succeed in 69.7% of episodes against 34.8% for retargeted hand poses.
- Human videos alone match 50 teleoperated demonstrations per task at about a third of the collection person-hours.

## Limitations
Each new robot still needs a few hours of teleoperated video to train the generative models and the IDM. Labels read from pixels carry no forces and are too imprecise for tasks like tight insertion. Human videos must come from the robot's fixed head-camera viewpoint. No method completes Air Fryer on the RB-Y1, and generating one 15 s demonstration takes about 11.6 minutes on an H100.
