abstract: 'Vision-based tactile sensors capture multiple aspects of physical interaction within a single image stream, including contact appearance, geometry, indentation depth, and tangential deformation. However, existing visuo-tactile policies often directly process tactile observations as images, making it difficult to determine which physical cues contribute to policy learning. This paper presents a controlled real-robot study that compares physically interpretable tactile signals using the LeRobot platform, enhanced with our visuo-tactile perception system. From the same tactile stream, we derive tactile RGB images, normal/depth maps, and marker-flow fields, representing contact appearance, geometry, and tangential deformation, respectively. Using identical task demonstrations and the same policy backbone, we evaluate the individual and combined contributions of these tactile cues. We further compare tactile encoders and visuo-tactile fusion strategies. Evaluation across 720 real-robot trials yields four key findings: (1) tactile perception substantially improves the overall success rate from 57.8% to 78.9%, although its contribution varies across task stages; (2) marker flow is the most effective individual cue, achieving a success rate of 74.4% and playing a particularly important role in regulating established contact; (3) cue-factorized encoding consistently improves performance, highlighting that different physical cues require tailored processing techniques; and (4) although the effectiveness of tactile fusion is task-dependent, cross-attention offers a lightweight solution with performance comparable to that of pretrained tactile encoders.'

## What they do
Vision-based tactile sensors carry several physical signals in one image stream, but policies usually treat that stream as a plain RGB image. This paper asks which signal actually helps imitation learning. From one sensor they derive three cue representations, tactile appearance, contact geometry and tangential deformation, and compare them on three real tasks with the same demonstrations and the same Diffusion Policy backbone.

## How it works
- **Platform.** A LeRobot-compatible parallel-jaw gripper with a GelSight Wedge-style sensor (marker-embedded elastomer, internal camera, calibrated lighting) on one fingertip, plus a global and a wrist camera.
- **Cues.** Tactile RGB is the raw image. A calibration network predicts a surface-normal map, which Poisson reconstruction turns into depth. Tracked marker displacements are interpolated into a dense flow field. "Full tactile" encodes all three with separate frontends.
- **Studies.** First the tactile input is varied against a vision-only baseline. Then, with full tactile fixed, ResNet-18 is compared with a tactile-pretrained Sparsh-DINO encoder, and concatenation with cross-attention fusion. Each configuration gets 30 trials per task.

## Results
- Full tactile succeeds in 71/90 trials versus 52/90 for vision only. Gains are 30.0 points on test-tube reorientation, 23.3 on wiping and 10.0 on peg insertion.
- Marker flow is the best single cue (67/90), ahead of tactile RGB (66/90) and normal/depth (60/90); full tactile beats the best single cue by one success per task.
- Cross-attention raises full-tactile success to 77/90 with ResNet-18 and 79/90 with Sparsh-DINO, so ResNet-18 with cross-attention roughly matches Sparsh-DINO with concatenation (75/90).
- Failure analysis suggests normal/depth helps establish contact, while marker flow helps regulate contact once it exists.

## Limitations
No direct force measurements, frontend architectures differ between cue configurations, and the study uses one tactile sensor, a parallel-jaw gripper and three tabletop tasks.
