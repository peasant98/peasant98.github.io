abstract: We introduce Berkeley Humanoid, a reliable and low-cost mid-scale humanoid research platform for learning-based control. Our lightweight, in-house-built robot is designed specifically for learning algorithms with low simulation complexity, anthropomorphic motion, and high reliability against falls. The robot's narrow sim-to-real gap enables agile and robust locomotion across various terrains in outdoor environments, achieved with a simple reinforcement learning controller using light domain randomization. Furthermore, we demonstrate the robot traversing for hundreds of meters, walking on a steep unpaved trail, and hopping with single and double legs as a testimony to its high performance in dynamical walking. Capable of omnidirectional locomotion and withstanding large perturbations with a compact setup, our system aims for scalable, sim-to-real deployment of learning-based humanoid systems. ## What they do
Berkeley Humanoid is a mid-scale humanoid research platform built almost entirely in-house: actuators, motor drivers, IMU, communication and power boards. It weighs 16 kg, stands 0.85 m tall, has two 6-DoF legs and costs about USD 10,000 without arms. The design goal is a narrow sim-to-real gap, so that a very simple RL controller is enough for robust outdoor locomotion.

## How it works
- **Simulation-friendly hardware.** No springs, dampers or closed kinematic chains. Custom quasi-direct-drive actuators (9:1 planetary gearbox, crossed-roller bearing) are mounted directly as joints; only the ankle pitch uses a linkage, with a linear joint-actuator mapping. EtherCAT keeps latency at 0.5-2 ms, so the actuators are simulated as delay-free torque sources, at more than 90,000 simulation steps per second on one GPU.
- **Reliable and cheap.** Aluminum and steel structure, cables routed through hollow shafts, and a cell-phone-grade IMU that costs less than a dollar.
- **Minimal controller.** An MLP actor (512, 256, 128) trained with PPO in Isaac Lab sees only the current state: no history, phase signal, reference motion or teacher-student training. It outputs joint position targets at 50 Hz.
- **Targeted randomization.** Mechanical parameters come from CAD and per-actuator friction tests, so their ranges are small; only contact properties and pushes are randomized widely. Motor strength and PD gains are not randomized.

## Results
- Walks on grass, brick, asphalt, stairs and unpaved trails, and recovers from kicks. It covered 364 m on campus in 10 minutes and climbed a trail with a 20 degree average incline for 96 m and 10.5 m of elevation gain.
- Velocity tracking error over 60 s: 0.051 m/s in simulation vs 0.058 m/s on hardware (sagittal), and 0.086 vs 0.1156 m/s (lateral).
- A hopping policy with the same setup except for rewards hops on two legs and on one leg (with a mostly slack safety rope).
- 38 recorded falls caused no hardware damage beyond two failures from loose screws and glue; resets take 3 to 5 seconds.

## Limitations
The robot has no arms yet. Range of motion, backlash, weight and mechanical strength need further hardware iterations. For more dynamic motion, the authors say the torque-current nonlinearity near saturation needs system identification, and motor operating region and heat protection should be simulated during training.
