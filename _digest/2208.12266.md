tldr: A convolutional network trained with a contrastive loss matches 3 s of MEG or EEG to wav2vec 2.0 features of the speech a person is hearing. Across 175 volunteers it picks the right segment out of more than 1,000 with up to 41% top-1 accuracy for MEG.
abstract: 'Decoding speech from brain activity is a long-awaited goal in both healthcare and neuroscience. Invasive devices have recently led to major milestones in that regard: deep learning algorithms trained on intracranial recordings now start to decode elementary linguistic features (e.g. letters, words, spectrograms). However, extending this approach to natural speech and non-invasive brain recordings remains a major challenge. Here, we introduce a model trained with contrastive-learning to decode self-supervised representations of perceived speech from the non-invasive recordings of a large cohort of healthy individuals. To evaluate this approach, we curate and integrate four public datasets, encompassing 175 volunteers recorded with magneto- or electro-encephalography (M/EEG), while they listened to short stories and isolated sentences. The results show that our model can identify, from 3 seconds of MEG signals, the corresponding speech segment with up to 41% accuracy out of more than 1,000 distinct possibilities on average across participants, and more than 80% in the very best participants - a performance that allows the decoding of words and phrases absent from the training set. The comparison of our model to a variety of baselines highlights the importance of (i) a contrastive objective, (ii) pretrained representations of speech and (iii) a common convolutional architecture simultaneously trained across multiple participants. Finally, the analysis of the decoder''s predictions suggests that they primarily depend on lexical and contextual semantic representations. Overall, this effective decoding of perceived speech from non-invasive recordings delineates a promising path to decode language from brain activity, without putting patients at risk for brain surgery.'

## What they do
Speech decoders that work well so far rely on electrodes implanted in the brain. This paper decodes *perceived* speech from non-invasive recordings instead. Given 3 s of MEG or EEG recorded while a healthy volunteer listens to stories or sentences, the model identifies which speech segment was heard among all segments of the test set. It is trained and evaluated on four public datasets, two MEG and two EEG, covering 175 participants.

## How it works
- **Speech module.** A wav2vec 2.0 model pretrained on 56k hours of speech turns each 3 s audio segment into a latent representation (the average of its last four transformer layers).
- **Brain module.** Raw M/EEG, resampled to 120 Hz, goes through a spatial attention layer over sensor positions, a subject-specific 1x1 convolution (the "Subject Layer") and five blocks of dilated residual convolutions. One model is trained across all participants of a dataset.
- **Contrastive loss.** Instead of regressing the Mel spectrogram, a CLIP loss aligns brain and speech latents, with the other segments in the batch as negatives. At test time the candidates are all test segments, so segments never seen in training can be decoded.
- **Minimal preprocessing.** Baseline correction, a robust scaler and clamping at 20 standard deviations.

## Results
- MEG: 70.7% (Gwilliams) and 67.5% (Schoffelen) top-10 segment accuracy out of more than 1,000 candidates, and up to 41.3% top-1; the best participant exceeds 80% top-1. A random model scores below 1% top-10.
- EEG is much lower: 25.7% (Brennan) and 17.7% (Broderick) top-10. The gap remains after matching sensors, subjects and recording time.
- On Gwilliams, top-10 rises from 12.4% (Mel regression) to 55.1% with the CLIP loss and 70.7% with wav2vec 2.0 targets.
- Removing the Subject Layer drops MEG top-10 to 42.4-47.0%; removing clamping collapses training. Accuracy grows with the number of training participants.

## Limitations
The model decodes speech perception in healthy listeners, not speech production in patients. The contrastive objective can only pick from a predetermined set of candidates, so a generative module is still needed. EEG performance is modest, zero-shot word decoding falls sharply for EEG, and MEG systems are not commonly deployed in clinics.
