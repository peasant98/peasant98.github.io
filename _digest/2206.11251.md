abstract: While behavior learning has made impressive progress in recent times, it lags behind computer vision and natural language processing due to its inability to leverage large, human-generated datasets. Human behaviors have wide variance, multiple modes, and human demonstrations typically do not come with reward labels. These properties limit the applicability of current methods in Offline RL and Behavioral Cloning to learn from large, pre-collected datasets. In this work, we present Behavior Transformer (BeT), a new technique to model unlabeled demonstration data with multiple modes. BeT retrofits standard transformer architectures with action discretization coupled with a multi-task action correction inspired by offset prediction in object detection. This allows us to leverage the multi-modal modeling ability of modern transformers to predict multi-modal continuous actions. We experimentally evaluate BeT on a variety of robotic manipulation and self-driving behavior datasets. We show that BeT significantly improves over prior state-of-the-art work on solving demonstrated tasks while capturing the major modes present in the pre-collected datasets. Finally, through an extensive ablation study, we analyze the importance of every crucial component in BeT. Videos of behavior generated by BeT are available at https://notmahi.github.io/bet

## What they do
Standard behavior cloning fits a unimodal policy with an MSE loss, which averages across the different ways demonstrators solve a task. BeT learns from offline demonstrations with no reward or task labels that mix several behavior modes, and samples among those modes at rollout instead of collapsing to one. It is evaluated in simulation on point-mass toys, CARLA driving from RGB images, multimodal block pushing with an XArm, and Franka Kitchen with 566 human VR demonstrations.

## How it works
- **Action discretization.** k-means over the dataset's actions gives k action centers. Each action becomes a bin index plus a residual, from which it is reconstructed exactly.
- **Transformer trunk.** A small minGPT (10^4 to 10^6 parameters) reads the last h observations and predicts a categorical distribution over bins at each timestep, trained with focal loss.
- **Action offsets.** A second head outputs a residual for each of the k bins. A masked multi-task loss trains only the residual of the ground-truth bin.
- **Sampling.** At test time BeT samples a bin from the predicted probabilities and adds that bin's residual.

## Results
- Block push (1,000 rollouts): BeT pushes one and two blocks into squares at 0.96 and 0.71. No baseline (MLP-MSE, nearest neighbor, LWR, VAE, Flow, IBC) exceeds 0.01 and 0.
- Kitchen: BeT completes 4 tasks in 44% of rollouts, against 24% for IBC. CARLA: 0.98 success, narrowly behind LWR at 1.0.
- Mode coverage: in CARLA BeT turns left/right 0.34/0.64 (demonstrations 0.50/0.50), while MLP, NN and LWR only turn right. Kitchen task-sequence entropy is 2.47, against 2.96 in the data and 2.41 for IBC.
- Ablations: without binning, Block push falls to 0.25 of full BeT performance; swapping the transformer for an MLP drops it to 0 and Kitchen to 0.05. BeT trains on Block push in under an hour, against about 14 h for IBC.

## Limitations
All experiments are in simulation. Rollouts are unconditional: there is no way yet to ask for one particular mode, which the authors leave to future work. Like other behavior cloning methods, BeT fails once observations drift out of distribution; in Block push its main failure is not noticing that a block has not fully entered the target. Mode frequencies are covered but not matched exactly, and some kitchen task sequences are over-visited.
