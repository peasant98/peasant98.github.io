tldr: T-Tac is a thin film sensor for a gripper that heats an object for 3 s and records how the contact temperature rises and falls, which differs from material to material. A classifier on that response tells 12 materials apart with 92.69% mean accuracy from a 10 s window.
abstract: Accurate material identification is important for robotic manipulation, yet vision-based methods face challenges when materials have similar appearances. Herein, we report T-Tac, a soft, thin, active thermal tactile sensor that identifies materials through their transient thermal responses. T-Tac integrates a polyimide film heater with an ionic conductive film temperature sensor, providing controlled thermal excitation and resistance-based temperature measurement in a film assembly suitable for mounting on robotic grippers. Single-sided transient plane source modeling and finite element simulations relate the thermal response to material thermal properties, while calibration and repeated measurements characterize the heating and sensing performance. Loading experiments at room and elevated temperatures further characterize the sensor response under an applied force. A hybrid classification framework concatenates extracted thermal descriptors with multiscale convolutional neural network features and uses an ExtraTrees classifier for material prediction. Experiments on 12 materials achieve a mean classification accuracy of 92.69% using a 3 s heating pulse and a 10 s observation window. These results support T-Tac as a compact approach to integrating active thermal material sensing into robotic end effectors. This work shed a light on the new approach for robotic material identification.

## What they do
Materials that look alike, such as different metals, are hard to tell apart by vision. Humans tell them apart by how warm or cold they feel. T-Tac copies this with an active thermal skin for robot grippers. A thin heater sends a short heat pulse into the touched object, and a soft temperature sensor underneath records how fast the contact heats and then cools. That response depends on the material's conductivity, density and heat capacity.

## How it works
- **Sensor.** A 20 x 20 x 0.15 mm polyimide film heater (10 Ω, driven from 5 V USB through a MOSFET) sits on a pectin-based ionic conductive film whose resistance falls as temperature rises. An ESP32-C6 microcontroller runs the heater and reads the resistance through an LCR module.
- **Physics model.** Single-sided transient plane source theory and COMSOL simulations show that low-conductivity materials (wood, PLA, TPU) reach peaks near 37-41 °C, while the nine metals stay near 25-26 °C and cool faster.
- **Classifier.** Hand-extracted thermal descriptors and multiscale 1D CNN features of the same temperature trace are combined into a 48-dimensional vector and classified with ExtraTrees.

## Results
- Sensor: 25-80 °C range, average sensitivity -1.46%/°C, R² 0.99 calibration fit, 4.10 s response time.
- 12 materials, 30 tests each (360 recordings), 5-fold cross-validation over 10 seeds. With a 3 s pulse and a 10 s window, fused features reach 92.69 ± 3.59%, against 91.56% for thermal features and 86.78% for CNN features alone. A 3 s window gives only 65.42%, and windows past 10 s add nothing.
- TPU is recognized 100% of the time. The main confusions are Cu/Fe and Ni/Zn, and 5.1% of PLA is called wood.

## Limitations
The accuracy numbers come from the sensor placed on top of same-sized cubes, not from grasps. The gripper-mounted runs are shown only as examples, without an accuracy. Each reading takes about 10 s. The authors leave objects of other sizes and surface conditions, and more materials, to future work.
