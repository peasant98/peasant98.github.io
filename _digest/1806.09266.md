## What they do
A grasp that is stable is not always a grasp that lets the robot use the tool. Dex-Net-style grasping picks the most robust grasp, often near the center of mass, but hammering wants the end of the handle and sweeping wants the head. TOG-Net jointly learns a task-oriented grasp and the manipulation action that follows it, trained only from simulated trial and error.

## How it works
- **Factored grasp score.** Task success probability is split into grasp quality (will the lift succeed?) times task quality given a successful grasp. 200 antipodal candidates from a depth image are scored by this product and refined with 3 iterations of the cross-entropy method.
- **Manipulation policy.** A Gaussian policy predicts the start pose (x, y, z, rotation) of a fixed open-loop motion primitive: a 40 cm straight sweep or a 90-degree wrist swing for hammering. It is trained with policy gradient, using task success as the reward.
- **Self-supervision.** One network with shared convolutional layers predicts all three outputs. It is trained in Bullet on 18,000 procedurally generated two-part tools (T-, L- and X-shapes), over three rounds of 500K trials with epsilon-greedy exploration.

## Results
- In simulation (100,000 episodes per method) the full model beats all four baselines on primitive, complex and realistic test objects for both tasks.
- Real world, 9 unseen objects, 270 trials: sweeping 71.1% vs 24.4% (antipodal + trained policy) and 23.6% (task-agnostic + trained policy); hammering 80.0% vs 33.3% and 44.4%.
- Miscellaneous objects unlike the training shapes are hardest: 60.0% sweeping and 66.7% hammering.

## Limitations
Grasps are restricted to top-down, 4-DoF, and manipulation is a single open-loop motion primitive per task. Training tools are two convex parts joined together, and the model sometimes misreads novel parts, e.g. sweeping round cans with a pan's round body. With a parallel-jaw gripper it grasps closer to the hammer head than ideal, since a far grasp is hard to balance.
