abstract: 'Learning for dexterous manipulation is limited by the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations: retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives no force feedback to the operator, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale up. We present PyRUA-Dex, a framework that enables coding agents to develop dexterous manipulation through programmatic policy, without the need of demonstrations. The programmatic policy is developed in two stages: during exploration, multiple agents explore in parallel for task skills and ways to compose them, and a distilling agent condenses the successful attempts into a task library and one program. During refinement, this program is improved through randomized layouts for enhanced robustness and execution efficiency. In simulated environments, PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex with a score of 72.9 and reaches 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization, ahead of every trained policy on both benchmarks. In real world experiments on a bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, PyRUA-Dex learns to play piano, write calligraphy, and other tasks efficiently: learning a new piece from sheet music takes as little as 30 minutes. These results suggest that PyRUA-Dex is an effective framework for developing dexterous manipulation policies with coding agents in the absence of high quality dexterous manipulation demonstrations.'

## What they do
Demonstrations for multi-fingered hands are scarce and tied to the hand they were recorded on. PyRUA-Dex does without them. Coding agents (Codex CLI sessions of GPT-6 Astra) develop a Python program for each task in simulation. The finished program runs frozen at the robot's control rate, with only cameras, joint positions and fingertip touch as input and no language model in the loop.

## How it works
- **One primitive library for every hand.** Generated from each robot's description file, it drives hands with 6 to 22 joints: shape the hand, grasp an object of measured size, close until held, check it is still held. Contact is read from fingertip tactile maps and joint lag, and objects are located with SAM 3.
- **Exploration.** Eight agents work on a task in parallel, each with its own layout and simulator checkpoints for retrying a stage. A shared memory passes on what worked, and a distilling agent picks the best program or assembles one from the most reliable stage functions.
- **Refinement.** One agent measures per-stage success over randomized layouts and repairs the weakest stage first. A new version is kept only if it does no worse on 32 validation layouts.
- **Real robot.** Both stages run in a MuJoCo model calibrated to the platform, with system-identified hand drives. After each round the program runs on the robot, and what goes wrong there is written back into the simulation.

## Results
- Bench2Dex (26 bimanual tasks, 12 embodiments): score 72.9 and 60.6% success, above every trained policy overall but ahead on only 15 of 26 tasks. Refinement raises success from 44.6% to 60.6%.
- At the benchmark's step budget, frozen programs complete 61.5% of Full-channel episodes, against 25.6% for the same model acting online.
- DexJoCo (Allegro hands): 81.0% success under full randomization versus 40.3% for π0.5.
- Real platform (two Franka FR3 arms, two 22-joint Sharpa hands): over refinement rounds, six tasks rise from 119 to 168 of 192 validation layouts in the calibrated simulation. On the robot the programs stack cubes, open a box, write "Dex" and play piano, and a new piano piece takes 30 minutes to an hour.

## Limitations
Programs handle geometry, order and timing well but sustained contact poorly. Trained policies win on the frying pan, the hand-to-hand pour and the jigsaw, and DexJoCo assembly reaches only 34.0%. Each task is learned separately, at a median of 427M tokens for exploration plus 214M for refinement. Real-robot reliability is measured in the calibrated simulation, not over repeated hardware trials.
