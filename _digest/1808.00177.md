## What they do
They train a Shadow Dexterous Hand (24 DoF, tendon-driven) to reorient an object in its palm to a sequence of goal orientations, using reinforcement learning only in simulation and no human demonstrations. Domain randomization, a recurrent policy and large-scale training let it transfer to the real hand, where human-like grasps and finger gaiting emerge without being rewarded.

## How it works
- **Policy.** An LSTM trained with PPO sees only fingertip positions (from motion capture) and the object pose, and outputs discretized relative joint targets at about 12 Hz. The value network also sees full simulator state (asymmetric actor-critic).
- **Randomization.** The MuJoCo model is calibrated, then randomized every episode: friction, masses, damping, actuator gains and gravity, plus observation noise, action noise and delay, backlash, marker dropouts and random forces on the object.
- **Scale.** OpenAI Five's distributed PPO: 384 16-core machines roll out, 8 GPUs optimize, about 2 years of simulated experience per hour.
- **Vision.** A CNN over three RGB cameras predicts object position and orientation, trained only on Unity renders with randomized appearance.

## Results
- Block, 10 real trials per policy, consecutive goals before failure: median 13 with motion capture, 28.5 with the wrist locked, 11.5 with vision (simulation: 50). Octagonal prism: median 5.
- Holding out randomizations on the real hand: median 0 with none, 2 without physics randomization, 2 without unmodeled effects. With vision, dropping observation noise cuts the median from 11.5 to 3.5.
- Feed-forward policies reach medians of 3 and 3.5, against 13 for the LSTM.
- Full randomization needs about 100 years of simulated experience (around 50 h) to match what 3 years (1.5 h) achieves without it.

## Limitations
A reality gap remains: real-hand performance is well below simulation. Each object gets its own policy (the prism policy is finetuned from the block one), and a sphere failed beyond a few rotations. Fingertips still need motion capture. The most common failure is dropping the object while the wrist pitches down, and hardware breakage was a key challenge.
