abstract: Generative robot policies based on diffusion and flow matching can model complex, multimodal behaviors, but remain susceptible to covariate shift during closed-loop execution. Existing approaches mitigate such failures through additional demonstrations, policy fine-tuning, or test-time guidance, but often require human intervention, engineered rewards, or other task-specific signals. We introduce GUARD, an anomaly-informed Q-steering framework that leverages the policy's training distribution to construct a dense, failure-anticipatory steering signal. GUARD pairs sparse task rewards with dense anomaly scores learned from policy training data and propagates both through a learned Q-function. Its gradients then steer the denoising process toward actions that improve task return while preventing trajectories from entering poorly supported regions, without directly modifying the pretrained policy. Across four Adroit dexterous manipulation tasks, GUARD achieves average success rates of $96.0\%$ and $80.0\%$ under nominal and perturbed dynamics, outperforming the strongest baseline by $21.0$ and $20.0$ percentage points while maintaining the lowest anomaly score. GUARD further demonstrates direct sim-to-real transfer on in-hand screwdriver rotation and reorientation tasks, achieving $40$ and $25$ percentage-point gains in success rate. These results demonstrate that anomaly signals can serve not only for failure monitoring but also to complement sparse task rewards as effective supervision for robust policy steering.

## What they do
Diffusion and flow-matching policies cloned from demonstrations drift into states their training data barely covers, and then fail. This is worst in contact-rich dexterous manipulation. GUARD keeps the pretrained policy frozen and nudges its denoising steps with the gradient of a learned Q-function. The Q-function is trained on a sparse success reward plus a dense anomaly score, so it predicts both task return and the risk of leaving the policy's support.

## How it works
- **Anomaly reward.** An anomaly detector (logpZO, a flow model fit to the demonstration observations) scores how poorly a state is supported. Each transition is rewarded for a low score at its next state, standardized and added with equal weight to the standardized sparse task reward. No failure examples or labels are needed.
- **Q-guided denoising.** At each flow step the clean action is estimated in one step, a pessimistic ensemble of five Q-functions is queried there, and its action gradient is added to the flow velocity.
- **Offline-to-online.** The Q-functions are initialized by fitted Q evaluation on noisy rollouts of the base policy, then adapted with rollouts of the steered policy.
- **Real robot.** For the 16-DoF Allegro V5 Plus hand, both stages run entirely in simulation and the result is deployed directly.

## Results
- Adroit (Relocate, Pen, Hammer, Door): 0.96 average success against 0.60 for the BC policy and 0.75 for DynaGuide, the best baseline. With action-noise perturbations: 0.80 against 0.41 and 0.60.
- Sparse reward alone gives 0.66 nominal; guiding directly with anomaly gradients (U-SA) gives 0.72.
- GUARD has the lowest average RND anomaly score in both settings.
- Real screwdriver tasks, 20 trials each: in-hand rotation goes from 20% to 60% success, with drops falling from 80% to 25%; palm-down reorientation goes from 70% to 95%.

## Limitations
On palm-up reorientation GUARD hurts: success falls from 30% to 10%. The authors attribute this to inaccurate contact modeling in simulation, which leaves the Q-function unaware of a failure mode seen on hardware. Q-function training needs online interaction, done here only in simulation. The real-robot policy sees joint positions and object pose; multimodal inputs are left to future work.
