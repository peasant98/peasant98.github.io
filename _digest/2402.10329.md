abstract: We present Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) -- a data collection and policy learning framework that allows direct skill transfer from in-the-wild human demonstrations to deployable robot policies. UMI employs hand-held grippers coupled with careful interface design to enable portable, low-cost, and information-rich data collection for challenging bimanual and dynamic manipulation demonstrations. To facilitate deployable policy learning, UMI incorporates a carefully designed policy interface with inference-time latency matching and a relative-trajectory action representation. The resulting learned policies are hardware-agnostic and deployable across multiple robot platforms. Equipped with these features, UMI framework unlocks new robot manipulation capabilities, allowing zero-shot generalizable dynamic, bimanual, precise, and long-horizon behaviors, by only changing the training data for each task. We demonstrate UMI's versatility and efficacy with comprehensive real-world experiments, where policies learned via UMI zero-shot generalize to novel environments and objects when trained on diverse human demonstrations. UMI's hardware and software system is open-sourced at https://umi-gripper.github.io.

## What they do
Hand-held grippers can collect demonstrations anywhere, but earlier systems only transferred simple grasping or quasi-static pick-and-place to robots. UMI pairs a 3D-printed hand-held gripper, with a GoPro as its only sensor, with a policy interface designed so the same data runs on different robots.

## How it works
- **Hardware.** A 155° fisheye lens gives visual context, and two side mirrors give implicit stereo, digitally reflected before training. Visual-inertial SLAM built on ORB-SLAM3 and the GoPro's IMU recovers metric 6-DoF poses under fast motion. Fiducial markers track the finger width continuously. Parts cost $73 plus $298 for the camera.
- **Same view on the robot.** Robots carry the same soft fingers and camera mount, so no camera-robot calibration is needed.
- **Latency matching.** Observation streams are aligned to the slowest one (usually the camera) using measured latencies, and actions are sent early to cover robot and gripper execution latency.
- **Relative actions.** Diffusion Policy outputs end-effector trajectories relative to the current pose. Bimanual policies also see the relative pose between the two grippers.

## Results
- Cup arrangement: 20/20, and 18/20 with the same checkpoint on a Franka FR2. Without the fisheye lens it scores 11/20, with absolute actions 5/20.
- Dynamic tossing lands 105/120 objects (87.5%) in the right bin, or 57.5% without latency matching. Bimanual sweater folding succeeds 14/20 (6/20 without inter-gripper pose). Seven-step dish washing succeeds 14/20.
- In the wild: 3 demonstrators collected 1400 demos at 30 locations in 12 person-hours. The policy succeeds 43/60 (71.7%) in two unseen environments, including unseen cups. Trained on lab data only, it scores 0%.
- SLAM against motion capture: 6.1 mm and 3.5° mean error. For cup arrangement, data collection is more than 3x faster than spacemouse teleoperation.

## Limitations
Each robot's kinematic limits are handled only by filtering demonstrations. SLAM needs textured scenes. Collecting data is still slower than demonstrating with a bare hand (48% and 64% of hand speed on the two tasks measured), because the gripper is heavy and has fewer degrees of freedom.
