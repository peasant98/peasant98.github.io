abstract: The problem of planning for a robot that operates in environments containing a large number of objects, taking actions to move itself through the world as well as to change the state of the objects, is known as task and motion planning (TAMP). TAMP problems contain elements of discrete task planning, discrete-continuous mathematical programming, and continuous motion planning, and thus cannot be effectively addressed by any of these fields directly. In this paper, we define a class of TAMP problems and survey algorithms for solving them, characterizing the solution methods in terms of their strategies for solving the continuous-space subproblems and their techniques for integrating the discrete and continuous components of the search.

## What they do
A manipulating robot must choose actions, their continuous parameters (which grasp, where to place an object) and collision-free motions. This review treats all three as one problem, task and motion planning (TAMP), and gives one framework for comparing the algorithms that solve it.

## How it works
- **Modes.** Picking or placing an object changes which degrees of freedom can move. Mode switches happen only where two modes' feasible sets intersect, usually a lower-dimensional set.
- **Plan skeletons.** Task-planning action templates get continuous parameters and constraints such as kinematics, stable placement, grasp, motion and collision-free. Fixing the action sequence leaves a hybrid constraint satisfaction problem (H-CSP) over the free parameters.
- **Solving H-CSPs.** Joint satisfaction optimizes all parameters at once. Individual satisfaction chains conditional samplers, for example a stable pose followed by IK for that pose.
- **Combining the searches.** Methods sequence first, satisfy first or interleave the two. They can also reuse solved constraints or small unsatisfiable sub-problems across skeletons.

## Results
- Table 1 places 33 lines of work (8 multi-modal motion planning, 25 TAMP) in a 3x3 grid. No listed method combines satisfaction-first with optimization.
- Sequencing first pays off when action sequencing is cheap and H-CSPs are expensive. Satisfying first pays off when sampling does not blow up combinatorially, each discrete search has real overhead, and sampled values rarely meet critical constraints.
- Joint satisfaction needs one solution per skeleton and lets constraints steer each other, but needs differentiable constraints, which collisions rarely provide. Samplers need only black-box checks but must cover the whole solution space.

## Limitations
Scope is limited to deterministic actions, fully known state, rigid objects of known shape and holonomic robots. There is no new algorithm or experiment, and the table is not exhaustive. Sampling and optimization solvers cannot prove a problem infeasible. Dynamics, deformables, uncertainty and learning are left as open directions.
