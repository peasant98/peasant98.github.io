abstract: 'Visual-inertial-leg (VIL) state estimation offers cost-effective localization for legged robots, adding only a camera to the onboard IMU and joint encoders. However, learning-based leg odometry is limited by its supervision: networks trained in simulation suffer from the sim-to-real gap, while real-world labels require costly sensors or inherit the errors of visual estimates. To address this, we present AirLeg, a self-supervised VIL SLAM system in which vision and leg proprioception improve each other from training to deployment. We pre-train the leg odometry network in simulation and adapt it to the robot without ground truth through imperative learning, where multi-sensor bundle adjustment uses the leg odometry to refine the visual map, which in turn supervises the network. During deployment, the fused estimates adapt the network online. To improve both localization and this supervision, a dual-window estimator fuses vision, IMU, and leg odometry, relates loaded feet to the visually reconstructed terrain through a deformation-aware constraint, and weights the constraints by variance component estimation. AirLeg completes all 17 GrandTour sequences with the lowest average error (0.19 m) among 19 methods. On a low-cost quadruped, it achieves the lowest drift (0.6%) with only 0.5 h of unlabeled data, in real time on an embedded computer.'

## What they do
Legged robots already carry an IMU and joint encoders, so adding a camera gives cheap visual-inertial-leg (VIL) localization. Learned leg odometry, however, either suffers a sim-to-real gap or needs real labels from motion capture, LiDAR or a visual estimator whose errors it then copies. AirLeg pretrains the leg odometry network in simulation and adapts it on the robot without ground truth, letting vision and leg proprioception correct each other.

## How it works
- **Leg odometry network.** A 0.85M-parameter Transformer reads 50 samples of IMU, joint states and load-normalized foot forces, and predicts body velocity, a gyroscope correction and uncertainties. It is pretrained on about 80 h of Isaac Lab data per robot with randomized gaits, terrains and robot and actuator parameters.
- **Self-supervised adaptation.** A bilevel (imperative learning) problem: multi-sensor bundle adjustment uses the leg odometry to refine the visual map, and the refined map supervises the network through reprojection errors. Only the heads and part of the backbone are trained.
- **Online estimator.** A short-window leg-aided stereo VO plus a long window that fuses leg, inertial, landmark and foot-terrain factors. The foot-terrain factor places each loaded foot on terrain reconstructed from stereo, with a slowly varying offset for sinkage. Factor weights are re-estimated online, and rank-1 adapters (about 1.3k parameters) keep adapting the network during deployment.

## Results
- GrandTour (ANYmal D, built-in IMU only, 17 test sequences, 18 baselines): mean ATE 0.15 m on normal and 0.23 m on challenging sequences. It is the only method under 0.4 m on all 17; every competitor fails or exceeds 4 m at least once.
- Unitree Go2 with a RealSense D455, ten sequences totaling 1.8 km: 0.59% average drift against 1.55% for the best competitor, OKVIS2. Three visual-inertial methods fail on the stairs.
- The simulation-only network gives 0.54 m mean ATE; 0.5 h of unlabeled data brings it to 0.23 m, 3.5 h to 0.19 m. Without supervision refinement, ATE rises from 0.189 m to 0.274 m.
- Runs in real time on the Go2's Jetson AGX Orin with 1.4 CPU cores and 2.6 GB of GPU memory.

## Limitations
On ice (ICE-1) and soft sand (RIV-1), AirLeg (0.31 m and 0.29 m) trails the best competitors (0.02 m and 0.13 m): leg odometry is unreliable on ice, and the leg-aided front end drifts on soft sand. Adaptation still needs real data from the target robot, and the global refinement with loop closure runs offline after a run.
