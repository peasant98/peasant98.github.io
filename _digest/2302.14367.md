tldr: A Transformer pretrained by masked spectrogram reconstruction on 43.7 hours of unannotated intracranial recordings from 10 subjects. A linear decoder on its fine-tuned embeddings reaches 0.83 average ROC-AUC on four audio and speech tasks, against 0.59 for a linear decoder on the raw signal.
abstract: We create a reusable Transformer, BrainBERT, for intracranial recordings bringing modern representation learning approaches to neuroscience. Much like in NLP and speech recognition, this Transformer enables classifying complex concepts, i.e., decoding neural data, with higher accuracy and with much less data by being pretrained in an unsupervised manner on a large corpus of unannotated neural recordings. Our approach generalizes to new subjects with electrodes in new positions and to unrelated tasks showing that the representations robustly disentangle the neural signal. Just like in NLP where one can study language by investigating what a language model learns, this approach opens the door to investigating the brain by what a model of the brain learns. As a first step along this path, we demonstrate a new analysis of the intrinsic dimensionality of the computations in different areas of the brain. To construct these representations, we combine a technique for producing super-resolution spectrograms of neural data with an approach designed for generating contextual representations of audio by masking. In the future, far more concepts will be decodable from neural recordings by using representation learning, potentially unlocking the brain like language models unlocked language.

## What they do
Linear decoders of neural recordings are explainable but weak; deep decoders are stronger but may solve the task themselves instead of reading it from the brain. BrainBERT sits in between: a Transformer is pretrained without labels on stereo-EEG (SEEG) recordings, one electrode at a time, and a linear decoder is then trained on its embeddings. Because it is subject- and electrode-agnostic, it can be used off the shelf on new subjects.

## How it works
- **Data.** 10 epilepsy patients (aged 4-19) watched feature-length movies while 1,688 electrodes were recorded at 2 kHz. After cleaning, 1,249 electrodes (4,551 electrode-hours) are cut into 5 s segments and pooled for pretraining.
- **Input.** Each segment becomes a spectrogram, either STFT or the superlet transform, z-scored per frequency bin so that no band dominates.
- **Pretraining.** A 6-layer Transformer encoder (12 heads, hidden size 768) reconstructs randomly masked time and frequency bands. For superlets the mask width adapts to frequency, and a content-aware loss adds weight on spectrogram elements far from zero.
- **Decoding.** A linear classifier reads the mean embedding over about 244 ms, with BrainBERT either frozen or fine-tuned.

## Results
- Four tasks on held-out sessions (sentence onset, speech vs. non-speech, pitch, volume), on the 10 electrodes where the raw linear decoder does best: fine-tuned BrainBERT (STFT) averages 0.83 ROC-AUC, against 0.63 for a 5-layer deep network and 0.59 for the linear decoder. Random initialization gives 0.60.
- Frozen BrainBERT (superlet) averages 0.63, on par with the deep network.
- Holding a subject out of pretraining changes that subject's decoding performance negligibly.
- On one superior temporal gyrus electrode, BrainBERT matches with 150 examples what the best baseline reaches with 1,000.

## Limitations
The released weights were pretrained only on passive movie viewing; the authors recommend pretraining from scratch if the downstream setting departs significantly from it. Decoding is evaluated on a subset of electrodes per task to limit compute, and all four tasks are binary classifications of the movie audio. The content-aware loss and adaptive mask each add only about 0.01 AUC.
