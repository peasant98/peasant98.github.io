abstract: Visual self-models learn from images how a robot moves under motor commands. Continuum robots need them even more, as they bend continuously without measured joint angles or fixed link geometry. Existing continuum self-models predict point clouds, image-space curves, or future images, but not the next 3D shape, appearance, and tip pose together from an observed body. 3D Gaussians offer a renderable representation of shape and appearance, yet articulated Gaussian self-models rely on joint angles to drive deformation. In this work, we propose BendGS, which uses a virtual backbone estimated from observation to drive command-conditioned Gaussian deformation, enabling next-state prediction for continuum robots without measured joint angles. In Gaussian modeling, a feed-forward network reconstructs the robot as 3D Gaussians from multi-view images, and the backbone, a chain of coordinate frames, is fitted to back-projected depth points to estimate the bending state. In transition, a network predicts its change under a relative motor command to deform the bound Gaussians. The deformed Gaussians render the next images and the 3D shape, and the distal frame gives the tip pose. Training losses use RGB-D images and masks, without bending or tip labels. On simulated and real robots, BendGS achieves the lowest Chamfer distance, best image quality, and lowest tip errors among the compared methods.

## What they do
Continuum robots bend continuously and have no measured joint angles, so their shape under a tendon command is hard to model. BendGS is a visual self-model that, from multi-view images of the robot's current state and a relative motor command, predicts the next 3D body shape, its rendered appearance and the tip position and pointing direction, all from one Gaussian representation.

## How it works
- **Gaussian modeling.** A feed-forward network (ReSplat) reconstructs the robot as 3D Gaussians from calibrated multi-view images. A virtual backbone, a chain of frames with two bending angles each, is fitted to points back-projected from the rendered depth, giving the bending state.
- **Binding.** Each Gaussian is rigidly bound to its nearest backbone frame; Gaussians far from the body are dropped as background.
- **Transition.** An MLP maps the fitted bending state and the command change to a bending increment. The frames move, the Gaussians follow, and the last frame gives the tip pose.
- **Training** uses only RGB images, depth maps and robot masks, with no bending or tip labels.

## Results
- Simulation: Chamfer distance 0.085 against 0.114 for the best baseline, 6.9 dB higher PSNR, and tip error 1.47 mm against 4.20 mm, with tilt 1.15° against 6.01°.
- Real three-module, nine-tendon robot with four RGB-D cameras: tip error 3.76 mm against 12.1 mm, tilt 4.16° against 16.3°, PSNR 15.83 dB against at most 12.35 dB. EMD was worse than two baselines.
- Ten chained predictions without a new observation: tip error 24.41 mm against 42.23 mm for predicting no motion.
- A prediction takes 0.83 s; once a state is observed, another command takes 3 ms.

## Limitations
Only quasi-static free motion after one command, from calibrated multi-view cameras. Real steps were small (4.52 mm average tip motion), so predicting no motion gave lower mean Chamfer distance and tilt. Contact, external loads, long horizons and closed-loop control are left for future work.
