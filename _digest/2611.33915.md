abstract: General-purpose multimodal foundation models enable robots to adapt during deployment from observations and execution feedback, without task-specific demonstrations or training. However, the robot retains what it learns only in the model's working context. Once the task ends, it loses this execution knowledge, such as how to carry a bowl between two grippers. On the next attempt at the task, the robot may take just as long and repeat the same mistakes. To avoid this repetition, robots must retain execution knowledge across attempts. Yet replaying past actions is insufficient, because their effects depend on the current conditions. Procedures that determine actions from the current scene address this, but when they meet conditions that earlier attempts did not cover, revising them requires the measured effects of earlier actions. We therefore propose SEEK, an agentic framework that retains both, enabling robots to improve across attempts while keeping the underlying model fixed. To adapt retained knowledge to the current scene, SEEK adopts a Self-Evolving Programmatic Policy (SEPP) that encodes manipulation as executable procedures and determines each action from current observations. As the robot executes these procedures, SEEK records the measured effects of its actions, including those from failed attempts, in a Persistent Action-Effect Bank (PAEB). Where a retained procedure applies, it acts without a model request; where it meets a condition it does not cover, this evidence guides its revision. On RoboDojo, knowledge retained over ten attempts raises SEEK's average task score from 27.0 to 54.4 and its success rate from 19.7% to 48.0% across all 42 tasks. On 34 of these tasks with GPT-6.2 Astra, SEEK reaches 80.1 after five attempts, against 49.6 for the same model acting directly, and uses 77.6% fewer model requests. https://zeyangguan.github.io/SEEK-Agentic-Robot-Manipulation/

## What they do
A general-purpose multimodal model can act as a robot policy without demonstrations or training, but what it learns during a task lives only in its context and is gone at the next attempt. SEEK keeps this execution knowledge across attempts in two linked forms, executable procedures and a record of measured action effects, so the robot improves while the model itself never changes.

## How it works
- **Self-Evolving Programmatic Policy (SEPP).** A main program plus named operations, written in restricted Python, that compute each action from the current RGB views and robot state. Objects are segmented and tracked with SAM3 and localized by triangulating across calibrated cameras; no depth or simulator state is used.
- **Persistent Action-Effect Bank (PAEB).** Every executed action is stored with its context and measured outcome, including those from failed attempts. Records are retrieved by ID, so each procedure version stays linked to the evidence it was written from.
- **Model in the loop only when needed.** Where a retained procedure applies it acts without a model request. When it asks for help or a fixed monitor detects stalls or oscillation, the model revises the procedure from PAEB records. After each attempt it keeps, replaces or reverts procedure versions.

## Results
- RoboDojo (simulated, 42 dual-arm tasks): knowledge from 10 attempts raises the task score from 27.0 to 54.4 and success from 19.7% to 48.0%, with gains on 37 tasks. That is the highest average among the listed methods, but below the VPP2 policy on Long-Horizon tasks (44.2 vs 56.3).
- With GPT-6.2 Astra on 34 tasks: 80.1 vs 49.6 for the model acting directly, at 14.1 instead of 63.2 model requests per episode.
- Ablation on 9 tasks: carrying procedures alone gives 47.5, evidence alone 57.3, both 70.4 (none: 30.3).
- Real Franka arm, four tabletop tasks, RGB only: the score rises from 66.9 to 83.3 against GPT-6 Astra acting directly, and requests per episode fall from 52.2 to 34.4.

## Limitations
Perception tools, executor and action interface are fixed, so performance still depends on them, including SAM3's accuracy. Knowledge is accumulated separately for each task, with no transfer between tasks yet. The tests cover only simulated dual-arm and real single-arm tabletop manipulation.
