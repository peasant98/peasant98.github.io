abstract: 'Centimeter-scale robots are promising for urban search and rescue because they can enter confined spaces that are inaccessible or unsafe for humans. However, their small size limits obstacle traversal after deployment. This paper presents the RESCUE Jumper, a 125 g mobile robot with a 6.2 cm width and 6.2 cm diameter wheels that combines ground rolling with jumping to traverse steps and gaps that exceeds its rolling capability. The RESCUE Jumper uses its two actuators for both rolling mode and jumping by reversing the direction of wheel motion. Forward wheel motion enables rolling, while reverse wheel motion winds a spring steel tail to store elastic potential energy and release it for jumping. The RESCUE Jumper varies tail winding speed to control jump direction, transitioning from backward to forward jumps as winding speed increases from 0.75 rad/s to 8.73 rad/s. The launch angle of the jumps ranges from 45 degree to -11 degree with respect to the vertical axis, reaching a maximum height of 9.2 cm and a maximum lateral displacement of 11.2 cm. We model the winding speed-dependent jumping behavior in three phases: tail release to determine the contact point and robot orientation, tail straightening until liftoff, and ballistic flight to predict jump height and lateral displacement. Finally, qualitative tests highlight the robot''s locomotion in cluttered, real-world environments. The model and experiments presented in this work can inform future designs of space-constrained jump mechanisms in centimeter-scale robots for search and rescue and inspection in unstructured environments.'

## What they do
RESCUE Jumper is a two-wheeled search-and-rescue robot small enough to pass through a 10 cm drilled hole. It rolls like a normal wheeled robot, but driving the wheels in reverse winds a spring-steel tail that releases and launches it, so jumping needs no actuator beyond the two drive motors. A three-phase model predicts jump direction, height and distance from the winding speed.

## How it works
- **Hardware.** Two Maxon brushless motors with 64:1 gearheads drive snail-cam wheels; an ESP32S3, three custom PCBs and two 1S LiPo batteries sit in the core. The tail is 100 mm of laser-cut spring steel.
- **Flexure-gate latch.** Rolling forward, rods on the wheels slide under passive flexure gates on the tail. In reverse they catch the tail, wrap it around the body and let go at its tip; the tail strikes the ground and throws the robot.
- **Direction from speed.** Winding speed changes where the tail hits the ground and how the body is tilted, which sets the launch direction.
- **Three-phase model.** Tail release, then the tail as an elastica beam straightening until liftoff (with hand-tuned damping), then ballistic flight.

## Results
- All 60 trials succeed (10 winding speeds from 0.75 to 8.73 rad/s, 6 each, filmed at 1000 fps). Launch angle ranges from 45° to -11° from vertical, with up to 9.2 cm height and 11.2 cm lateral displacement.
- Chaining the three phases gives RMSEs of 21.2° in launch direction, 13.5 mm in peak height and 57.2 mm in landing distance. It predicts the backward-to-forward switch at 1.44 rad/s; it happens at 4.51 rad/s.
- Teleoperated outdoors, the robot jumped from gravel onto a 5 cm ledge it could not roll over.

## Limitations
Jumps are tested on nonslip grip tape, from a manually set pre-jump orientation, with no onboard sensing. The model is sensitive to center-of-gravity and body-flex errors, and its error accumulates across phases. At low battery voltage the tail fails to release. Jump control and higher jumps are future work.
