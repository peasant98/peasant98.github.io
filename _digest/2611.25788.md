abstract: 'A collision-free detour can still bring a legged robot to the next stair or ledge a few centimetres or degrees off, and a fixed locomotion skill, factory or learned, can stall or fall. Existing methods prevent this with a robot model or extensive trial and error, but on a new site with unknown skills neither is available, so they cannot be used. We find that a single 27-minute teaching session already supplies what these methods have to derive or learn: for every stair and ledge, one crossing the skill has completed. Our method, passage-conditioned navigation, makes the route remember how each transition was entered, not only where the path went. It stores each taught crossing as an event and, without training or a robot model, enforces it after every local replan: before each ledge, an entry gate makes the robot turn or back up and retry if it is more than 10 cm or 10° off the taught crossing. This suits any robot with fixed skills that repeats a route over terrain transitions, as in inspection and patrol, where one wrong entry can end the task. ## What they do
Wheeled-legged robots cross stairs and ledges with fixed locomotion skills that succeed only from a limited set of entry states. A collision-free detour can deliver the robot to a ledge a few centimetres or degrees off, and the skill stalls or falls. Passage-conditioned navigation takes the entry condition from one teaching session (27 minutes of driving) instead of a robot model or extra trials, and enforces it after every local replan. Nothing is trained on site.

## How it works
- **Stored route.** Teaching pose trails, waypoint marks and gait events compile into a route of 270.9 m with 29 waypoints. Each stairs or ledge transition is an event placed by distance along the route, with a gait interval and a speed cap; each ledge also keeps the position and direction of its taught crossing.
- **Approach window and entry gate.** Near a ledge the robot slows and tracks the stored line closely. 0.7 m before the crossing it checks its pose in the crossing frame; if it is more than 0.10 m or 10° off, it turns in place or backs up and approaches again.
- **Local repair.** A LiDAR local planner detours around unmapped obstacles inside a corridor; a displaced or stalled robot walks forward while steering back to the stored line.

## Results
- Kinematic simulation, offsets injected 0.76 m before each ledge: conventional tracking keeps 22.4% of crossings within both limits, the approach window 47.6%, the gate 100%. Under localization noise: 24.1%, 46.0% and 88.6%. Time from offset to crossing rises from 4.2 s to 11.3 s.
- Contact simulation with learned policies: no falls in 180 stair climbs after a push, against 9 when the stored direction is ignored. After a 1.2 m push, 75.7% of segments are completed with rejoining (0.4% without), matching undisturbed runs (77.4%).
- Real robot with factory gaits: all six competition-day runs of the route, which spans 7.0 m of elevation, completed autonomously, placing third in its task.

## Limitations
The gate is conservative: of approaches made without it that fail its check, 50.8% still crossed within the limits. Skill choice and stepping over obstacles are explicit hand-set rules. On stairs and around ledges the planner uses the stored route instead of live sensing, which covers 58% of the route. The simulation studies use the authors' learned policies, not the robot's factory gaits.
