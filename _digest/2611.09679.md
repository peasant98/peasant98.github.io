abstract: Particle image velocimetry (PIV) reconstructs a flow field from the motion of tracer particles but the reconstruction quality is bounded by how well the flow structure of interest is resolved in the images. The acquisition position is typically fixed by the operator before the measurement. We cast camera placement as a sequential decision-making problem and train a reinforcement learning agent to control a 6-DoF manipulator equipped with a camera, enabling it to locate, frame and resolve a hydrofoil wake in a water tunnel. The agent commands the camera pose and observes the scene only through the velocity field and uncertainty estimated from time-separated image pairs. It is trained entirely in a photorealistic simulation of our water tunnel, with wakes generated by Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations of hydrofoils with varying profiles, positions and angles of attack. After zero-shot transfer to the real water tunnel, the policy reliably locates and frames the wakes of randomly placed hydrofoils. It resolves the wake at a higher resolution than a fixed camera view and with lower error in the measured flow statistics, in roughly half the time of a full scan and with far fewer image pairs.

## What they do
In particle image velocimetry (PIV), the camera position is usually fixed before the measurement, so a flow feature can fall outside the frame or be poorly resolved. This paper treats camera placement as an RL problem. A camera on a 6-DoF arm must locate a hydrofoil wake in a water tunnel, frame all of it, and get as close as it can. The policy is trained only in simulation and deployed zero-shot on the real tunnel.

## How it works
- **Observation and action.** Each step, the camera records four image pairs. LIMA, a CNN-based PIV estimator, turns them into velocity, velocity-deficit and uncertainty fields. The policy outputs a Cartesian camera position 0.25-0.60 m from the light sheet, with the optical axis kept normal to the sheet. Without the uncertainty input, policies often framed the hydrofoil's shadow or unlit corners.
- **Reward.** Computed from CFD ground truth in simulation only: the product of the fraction of the wake in view and the fraction of the frame it fills, normalized so that the closest pose capturing the whole wake scores one.
- **Simulation.** An Isaac Sim replica of the lab with path-traced lighting and shadows, and tracers advected through 719 lattice-Boltzmann CFD cases. Seeding density, tracer size and sensor noise are randomized. The agent is unmodified DreamerV3.
- **Deployment.** A calibration table built from an ArUco board in the water maps each commanded view to joint angles.

## Results
- Simulation: the median error between the settled and required view is below 10 mm in x and y and about 20 mm in distance. Per-step reward is more than twice that of the best fixed camera. From the required pose, LIMA recovers 72% of the true wake region, against 54% from 520 mm.
- Real tunnel, two hydrofoil profiles unseen in training, six settings each: the policy frames the whole wake in 41 of 42 views (fixed wide view: 7 of 11), at 0.090 mm per pixel versus 0.136 mm.
- Compared with a 15-tile scan mosaic, the velocity-deficit error falls from 0.419 to 0.205. An episode takes a median of 85 s and 160 image pairs, against 148 s and 1500 pairs for the scan.

## Limitations
The policy does not set exposure, inter-frame time or aperture. Moving to another tunnel needs accurate CAD models, lighting that simulation reproduces well, and extensive camera calibration. Hardware tests used one small tunnel and two hydrofoil profiles.
