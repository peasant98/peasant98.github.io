abstract: 'Developing exoskeleton controllers that generalize across diverse locomotor conditions typically requires extensive motion-capture data and biomechanical labeling, limiting scalability beyond instrumented laboratory settings. Here, we present a physics-based neuromusculoskeletal learning framework that trains a hip-exoskeleton control policy entirely in simulation, without motion-capture demonstrations, and deploys it on hardware via policy distillation. A reinforcement learning teacher policy is trained using a muscle-synergy action prior over a wide range of walking speeds and slopes through a two-stage curriculum, enabling direct comparison between assisted and no-exoskeleton conditions. In simulation, exoskeleton assistance reduces mean muscle activation by up to 3.4% and mean positive joint power by up to 7.0% on level ground and ramp ascent, with benefits increasing systematically with walking speed. On hardware, the assistance profiles learned in simulation are preserved across matched speed-slope conditions (r: 0.82, RMSE: 0.03 Nm/kg), providing quantitative evidence of sim-to-real transfer without additional hardware tuning. These results demonstrate that physics-based neuromusculoskeletal simulation can serve as a practical and scalable foundation for exoskeleton controller development, substantially reducing experimental burden during the design phase.'

## What they do
Exoskeleton controllers that work across speeds and slopes are usually trained on motion-capture and force-plate data. This paper instead trains a hip-exoskeleton policy entirely in a predictive neuromusculoskeletal walking simulation, with no motion-capture demonstrations. It then distills the policy into an IMU-only controller for a custom hip exoskeleton.

## How it works
- **Simulation.** A 21-DoF, 90-muscle human model in Hyfydy/SCONE, with bilateral hip torque actuators (up to 12 Nm) and 4.3 kg of added device mass.
- **Teacher.** SAC with full simulator state. It acts through 10 muscle-synergy coefficients per leg (from NMF on human walking data), 10 trunk activations and 2 hip torques, at 0.7-1.5 m/s and slopes of -5° to +5°.
- **Curriculum.** The model first learns to walk unassisted for 5×10^7 steps. Training then continues in two matched branches, with the exoskeleton and with it clamped to zero.
- **Student.** A temporal convolutional network maps 0.95 s of thigh mediolateral gyroscope signal to hip torque. It is trained by behavioral cloning and runs at 100 Hz on a Jetson Orin Nano.

## Results
- Simulated gait matches open-source human data with mean RMSE 7.32° for joint angles and 0.264 Nm/kg for moments.
- In simulation, assistance lowers muscle activation by 1.1% (level) and 3.4% (ramp ascent), and positive joint power by 6.3% and 7.0%. Ramp descent benefits less, and the reduction grows with speed.
- With five treadmill participants, hardware torque waveforms match simulation with r = 0.82 ± 0.19 and RMSE 0.03 Nm/kg. Agreement ranges from r = 0.66 on ramp descent to 0.98 on ramp ascent.
- Torque peaks lag the biological hip moment by 103 ms (extension) and 166 ms (flexion). These delays emerge from training instead of being hand-set.

## Limitations
The human study was not an efficacy test: only torque and ground reaction forces were logged, so it does not show reduced effort for real users. Only cyclic walking is covered. A single gyroscope axis may not be enough for broader tasks and may be sensitive to sensor placement. Domain randomization was not tuned for sim-to-real.
