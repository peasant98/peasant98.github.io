abstract: In this work, we introduce OmniFish, a bioinspired modular soft robotic fish designed to emulate the full spectrum of body and/or caudal fin (BCF) locomotion modes within a single robotic platform. The design combines morphology averaging across representative fish species with a tendon-driven compliant segmented spine whose effective stiffness can be reconfigured through progressive mechanical jamming. By selectively constraining body segments, OmniFish shifts deformation from whole-body undulation to posterior body bending and localized tail oscillation, enabling bioinspired Anguilliform, Subcarangiform, Carangiform, Thunniform, and Ostraciiform swimming using a single tendon-driven actuator. The design was guided by morphometric analysis and evaluated through caudal-fin thrust characterization, numerical body-drag simulations, kinematic analysis, and swimming performance experiments. Kinematic analysis confirmed the intended robot transition from distributed body undulation to localized tail oscillation. Swimming experiments demonstrated distinct mode-dependent performance, with the Ostraciiform configuration achieving the highest straight-line velocity of 8.14 cm/s and the lowest cost of transport of 2.42 at 1.5 tail beats/s. These proof-of-concept results show that a mechanically simple stiffness-reconfigurable architecture can generate distinct BCF swimming modes and provide a versatile strategy for developing multimodal soft robotic fish.

## What they do
Most robotic fish are designed around one swimming style. OmniFish is a soft robotic fish, 40 cm long plus its tail fin, meant to cover all five body and caudal fin (BCF) modes: Anguilliform (eel), Subcarangiform (trout), Carangiform (mackerel), Thunniform (tuna) and Ostraciiform (boxfish). One servo drives the body, and the mode is set by mechanically locking spine segments.

## How it works
- **Averaged shape.** Body, fin and cross-section outlines of the five species are traced, normalized and averaged point by point. In CFD the averaged body had drag close to the tuna body and a smaller drag increase in 10° off-axis flow than the trout, mackerel and tuna bodies.
- **Fin selection.** 36 3D-printed caudal fins (6 shapes, 3 material combinations, 2 thickness profiles) were tested on a thrust rig, 9,072 measurements in all. The averaged fin with a tapered profile gave the highest thrust, 0.256 N at 7 tail beats/s.
- **Actuation.** A Dynamixel servo in the rigid head pulls two antagonistic nylon tendons along a five-segment compliant lattice spine under a silicone skin.
- **Mode switching.** Jamming blocks lock segments 1, 1-2, 1-3 or 1-4, moving bending toward the tail; no locks gives the eel-like mode.

## Results
- Kinematics at 1 tail beat/s: as more segments were locked, the point of maximum curvature moved from 0.54 to 0.79 body lengths and the head-to-tail amplitude ratio rose from 0.71 to 1.01.
- 120 swimming trials on a linear rig (5 modes, 4 frequencies, 6 repetitions). The three most flexible modes peaked at 1 tail beat/s with 2.09-2.60 cm/s. Thunniform reached 3.55 cm/s and Ostraciiform 8.14 cm/s (0.179 body lengths/s), both at 1.5 tail beats/s. Cost of transport fell from 7.94 (Subcarangiform) to 2.42 (Ostraciiform).
- The Ostraciiform mode had a lower cost of transport than Tunabot, and every mode had a lower one than SoFi. A tensegrity multimodal fish was much faster and more efficient.

## Limitations
The robot is tethered and rides on a linear guide, and its speeds stay far below the biological range it was designed for (Strouhal numbers 1.38-10.24). The servo caps the tail-beat frequency at 2 per second, although the fin produced its peak thrust at 7. Switching modes means removing the skin by hand, the body wave has local amplitude dips at segment transitions, and turning was not tested.
