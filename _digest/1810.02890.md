abstract: Imitation learning has proven to be useful for many real-world problems, but approaches such as behavioral cloning suffer from data mismatch and compounding error issues. One attempt to address these limitations is the DAgger algorithm, which uses the state distribution induced by the novice to sample corrective actions from the expert. Such sampling schemes, however, require the expert to provide action labels without being fully in control of the system. This can decrease safety and, when using humans as experts, is likely to degrade the quality of the collected labels due to perceived actuator lag. In this work, we propose HG-DAgger, a variant of DAgger that is more suitable for interactive imitation learning from human experts in real-world systems. In addition to training a novice policy, HG-DAgger also learns a safety threshold for a model-uncertainty-based risk metric that can be used to predict the performance of the fully trained novice in different regions of the state space. We evaluate our method on both a simulated and real-world autonomous driving task, and demonstrate improved performance over both DAgger and behavioral cloning.

## What they do
DAgger collects expert labels while control switches at random between the expert and the learner (the novice). With a human expert this hurts safety and label quality: the human gets little feedback from their own inputs and perceives actuator lag. In HG-DAgger (Human-Gated DAgger) the human decides when to take over and keeps uninterrupted control until handing back. It is tested on weaving a car between stationary obstacles, in simulation and on a real vehicle.

## How it works
- **Human gating.** The novice drives until the expert judges the state unsafe, takes over (on the car, by turning the wheel), recovers, then returns control with a button. Only these recovery segments are labeled and added to a dataset that starts from behavioral cloning data.
- **Doubt.** The novice is a neural-network ensemble; its doubt is the l2 norm of the diagonal of the ensemble's output covariance.
- **Learned threshold.** Doubt is logged at each intervention, and the threshold is the mean of the last 25% of those values. States below it form the estimated safe set.
- **Setup.** A low-dimensional state (lane position, heading, speed, distances to lane edges and obstacles) maps to steering and speed. Every method starts from 10,000 cloning labels and adds 2,000 per epoch for five epochs.

## Results
- Simulation: HG-DAgger learns faster and more stably than DAgger and behavioral cloning; DAgger turns unstable in later epochs as its expert share decays.
- Rollouts started inside the estimated safe set had a 12x lower collision rate and 20x lower road-departure rate than those started outside; departures lasted 1.63 s vs 3.74 s.
- Real car: 0 collisions and 0 road departures, versus 1 and 6 for behavioral cloning and 1 and 1 for DAgger. Steering was 21.1% closer to the human's distribution than DAgger's.
- Over 40 obstacle layouts, the learned threshold was near-optimal for separating free from occupied space.

## Limitations
The authors say the small amount of on-vehicle test data limits statistical significance. Obstacles on the real car were simulated, the policy sees a hand-designed state, and the risk threshold is only used for evaluation, not to gate the policy at test time. Training-time safety relies on the human intervening.
