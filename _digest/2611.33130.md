abstract: Robot policies predominantly learn from human-collected data emphasizing competent behavior, limiting coverage of the low-level behaviors needed to support autonomous improvement. We study how a form of midtraining can be used to build coverage over diverse low-level behaviors that connect challenging states of the robot and its environment to states from which further progress is possible ("connected behavioral coverage"). We also examine how this coverage supports downstream adaptation and online improvement. To this end, we introduce $β$-Data, a dataset collected via adversarial teleoperation, where a human adversary reversibly perturbs the environment while a human operator continues toward completion without resetting. We relabel trajectory segments with local outcomes in natural language and finetune a pretrained policy conditioned on recent action history. The resulting policy exhibits sustained use of more low-level behaviors. Across manipulation tasks in simulation and on three real-robot platforms, our approach yields more favorable data scaling laws, better zero-shot generalization, and more effective use of additional rollout time than training on standard demonstrations. During online finetuning, these policies need fewer rollouts to collect successful experience and reach higher success rates, suggesting that midtraining can prepare robots for both more robust task execution and continued autonomous improvement. Our project page and robot videos are available at https://kshitiz-1225.github.io/exploratory-midtraining-robot-policies/.

## What they do
Teleoperated demonstrations mostly show competent execution, so policies trained on them see few of the retries, regrasps and rearrangements needed once something goes wrong. The authors collect β-Data with adversarial teleoperation: an operator works toward the goal while an adversary reversibly perturbs the scene or the task (returns a packed item to the table, rejoins bricks), and the operator carries on without resetting. They midtrain π0.5 on it and compare against the same hours of standard demonstrations, in simulated bimanual packing and on three real bimanual platforms.

## How it works
- **Coverage metric.** "Connected behavioral coverage" counts distinct directed transitions between abstract states, such as object locations. At matched budgets β-Data has 85 vs 54 in simulated packing and 125 vs 72 in real packing.
- **Segment relabeling.** Each episode is cut into segments labeled with their local outcome in language; at test time a high-level planner supplies the current subtask.
- **Action history.** The policy sees its last 120 actions, relative to the current state and encoded as four tokens, so it can tell a first attempt from a retry.
- **Online finetuning.** Filtered behavior cloning on the policy's own successful rollouts, mixed with offline data.

## Results
- Real robot, held-out configurations, 20 trials each: LEGO interlock 19/20 vs 5/20, xArm packing 12/20 vs 4/20, pants 14/20 vs 3/20, T-shirt 9/20 vs 5/20.
- Simulation, long-horizon held-out tasks: 57.6% vs 39.2%. 2.6 h of β-Data reaches 60.3%, about the 58.7% from twice as many hours of standard demonstrations.
- Online finetuning on the long held-out sim tasks: 57.6% to 73.2% using 160 rollouts on average, versus 39.2% to 52.0% using 3,810 for standard demonstrations. On the real xArm, success rises from 12/20 to 17/20; the standard-demonstration policy stays at 20%.

## Limitations
Each episode needs two people, and the perturbations vary with whoever plays the adversary. At the smallest budget (0.65 h) standard demonstrations do better (16.5% vs 6.0%), both policies stay at or below 16% on the hardest held-out rearrangement tasks, and the two-assembly LEGO task reaches only 6/20. Whether simply scaling ordinary demonstrations would give the same benefit is left open.
