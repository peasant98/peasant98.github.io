abstract: Flipping is a fundamental robotic manipulation skill that requires reorienting a target object in a stable pose on a planar support surface by rotating it 180$^\circ$ about an axis parallel to the support surface -- think of flipping a coin from heads to tails. Although flipping is a common objective in industrial tasks such as parts inspection, mechanical assembly, and tool use, coding a bimanual robot with grippers to flip diverse objects is a significant challenge. In this paper, we show that a coding agent can autonomously develop and refine object-flipping strategies through iterative learning in physics simulations across diverse object geometries. Progressive skill acquisition, from basic handover to toppling and pushing, robust grasping and sensing, and thin-object manipulation with recovery, improves task success from 24.7% to 95.0%. The resulting graph-based code integrates high-level strategy selection with reactive control, enabling multi-stage flipping on physical robots.

## What they do
Flipping an object 180 degrees on a table needs different strategies for different shapes: a hammer can be handed between two grippers, a wide multimeter has to be pushed and toppled. FlipAnything is a short benchmark and case study in which a coding agent (Claude Fable 5.1 in the Graph-as-Policy harness) writes one program that flips 24 objects with bimanual YAM arms, improving it only through simulated trials, without demonstration data.

## How it works
- **Inputs.** An English task description, CAD models of the 24 objects and the arm URDF. Simulation uses RoboSimStudio and Newton with a MuJoCo-based solver and CoACD convex decomposition.
- **Self-learning loop.** The agent starts with a handover-only graph, evaluates it on all objects at randomized positions, reads success rates, traces and failure modes (infeasible grasps, lost contact, failed handovers, wrong orientation estimates), then revises perception, grasp selection, primitives, strategy selection and recovery.
- **Learned artifact.** A graph that converts to executable code driven by RGB-D and proprioception. It picks between handover, repositioning, stand-and-topple and pushing, with closed-loop recovery such as grip verification and extra pushes.

## Results
- Over 360 episodes per iteration (24 objects, 15 each), success goes from 24.7% (89/360) with handover only to 80.6% (290/360) after adding toppling and pushing, and to 95.0% (342/360) for the best controller.
- Per object: claw hammer 14/15 by in-air handover, both lock nuts and the multimeter 15/15, tape measure 14/15, pipe wrench 13/15, surgical scissors from 0/15 to 13/15.
- Real robot, zero-shot on three unseen objects: clamp 2/3, blue tape 3/3, foldable helix screwdriver 1/3.

## Limitations
A short paper that reports no baselines. The real-world test is three objects with three trials each, and the authors leave generalization to unseen objects to future work. The 95.0% is measured on the same 24 objects the code was refined on.
