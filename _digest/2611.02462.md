abstract: 'Treating morphology and control as separate design problems has constrained the efficiency and adaptability of quadrupedal robots, while current co-design approaches are often constrained by restricted design spaces. We overcome this limit by introducing Dogger, a bio-inspired quadruped with modular three-segment legs, four-bar linkages and adjustable passive knee compliance. A two-stage framework combining reinforcement learning and Bayesian optimization is developed: the morphology-conditioned control policy is trained across designs and then held fixed during task-specific design selection. The resulting designs and policies deployed zero-shot reduce the cost of transport by 18.6% and 35.7% for inspection and payload-carrying tasks. Comparisons demonstrate that full geometry-compliance co-design outperforms fixed and restricted design scopes. Cross-task tests further reveal distinct task-specific morphologies with shared mechanical trade-offs. These results provide hardware-level evidence that expanding the physical design space alongside learned control is essential to realize the full potential of task-driven morphology-control co-design in legged robotics.'

## What they do
Co-design of legged robots often searches a narrow mechanical space, such as knee elasticity alone. Dogger is a 6.5 kg quadruped with three-segment legs and a parallel knee spring; link lengths and spring settings change by reprinting or swapping parts. A two-stage RL plus Bayesian optimization (BO) pipeline picks a leg design for each task, and the design and policy go to hardware without retraining.

## How it works
- **Design space.** Five parameters: three link-length scale factors (0.7-1.3), the spring wheel diameter (0-50 mm, sets knee stiffness) and the spring offset (0-10 mm, sets where the spring engages).
- **Universal policy.** One PPO policy takes the design parameters as input and trains across randomly sampled designs in up to 8,192 parallel environments. Training takes 15 min to 2 h on an RTX 4070.
- **Design selection.** With the policy frozen, BO (Optuna's TPE solver) finds the design with the lowest cost of transport for the task. The energy model counts motor losses and the constant power draw of the electronics.
- **Sim-to-real.** Domain randomization, observation noise, velocity disturbances and a feedforward torque for the knee spring. The policy runs at 50 Hz on a Jetson Orin Nano.

## Results
- Inspection walking: the selected design lowers cost of transport by 12.6% in simulation and 18.6% ± 1.7% on hardware, relative to the nominal design with springs disengaged. With a 2 kg payload: 34.0% in simulation, 35.7% ± 3.1% on hardware.
- Each design also helps on the other task (13.5% and 23.7% on hardware), but less than the design selected for that task.
- In simulation, link lengths alone give 1.1% (inspection) and 24.0% (payload) and springs alone give 7.3% and 16.3%. Optimizing both gives the largest gain on both tasks.
- A CPG + CMA-ES co-design baseline is 4.0% better on inspection but 3.2% worse with the payload; privileged RL + BO is 12.8% and 3.7% worse. Outdoors, the robot walked on gravel, tall grass, soil and forest debris, and one continuous 17-minute trial ran without a fall.

## Limitations
Designs are selected under a fixed, pretrained policy, so the chosen morphology depends on what that controller can do. Quantitative comparisons are on flat ground only, with two tasks and five design variables. About half the measured energy goes to the electronics, which no leg design can reduce.
