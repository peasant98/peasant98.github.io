abstract: 'Deep reinforcement learning has emerged as a promising and powerful technique for automatically acquiring control policies that can process raw sensory inputs, such as images, and perform complex behaviors. However, extending deep RL to real-world robotic tasks has proven challenging, particularly in safety-critical domains such as autonomous flight, where a trial-and-error learning process is often impractical. In this paper, we explore the following question: can we train vision-based navigation policies entirely in simulation, and then transfer them into the real world to achieve real-world flight without a single real training image? We propose a learning method that we call CAD$^2$RL, which can be used to perform collision-free indoor flight in the real world while being trained entirely on 3D CAD models. Our method uses single RGB images from a monocular camera, without needing to explicitly reconstruct the 3D geometry of the environment or perform explicit motion planning. Our learned collision avoidance policy is represented by a deep convolutional neural network that directly processes raw monocular images and outputs velocity commands. This policy is trained entirely on simulated images, with a Monte Carlo policy evaluation algorithm that directly optimizes the network''s ability to produce collision-free flight. By highly randomizing the rendering settings for our simulated training set, we show that we can train a policy that generalizes to the real world, without requiring the simulator to be particularly realistic or high-fidelity. We evaluate our method by flying a real quadrotor through indoor environments, and further evaluate the design choices in our simulator through a series of ablation studies on depth prediction. ## What they do
Learning collision avoidance by trial and error is impractical on a fragile quadrotor. CAD2RL trains a vision-based policy entirely in simulated 3D hallways and flies it on real drones without ever seeing a real image. Instead of making the renderer realistic, it randomizes textures, lighting and furniture so heavily that real hallways look like one more variation.

## How it works
- **Policy.** A VGG16-based fully convolutional network maps one monocular RGB image to a 41x41 grid of bins; each bin is a flight direction through that part of the image, and the drone greedily picks the bin with the highest Q-value.
- **Training.** The network is first pretrained to predict free space (a 1 m raycast per bin). It is then finetuned with Monte Carlo policy evaluation: from random states, every action is rolled out with the current policy for 5 steps, and the network regresses onto the resulting returns. Reward is 0 at a collision and otherwise grows with distance to the nearest obstacle, capped at 1.
- **Simulator.** 24 Blender hallways from 12 floorplans, 21 furniture items and 200 wall textures, with random lights and camera height and yaw.

## Results
- In a realistic scanned hallway (60 fixed starts), CAD2RL keeps 1.2 km of collision-free flight in about 40% of runs, ahead of free-space prediction alone, a left-right-straight classifier and a straight-line controller.
- Real hallway following on Parrot Bebop drones: 6 collisions over 164 m (27.3 m mean safe flight) versus 13 over 162 m (12.5 m) for free-space prediction in Cory Hall; 4 versus 7 collisions in SDH.
- On 620 real RGB-D images, free-space prediction trained on randomized hallways beats fixed-texture training and training on a realistic reconstruction of the same building.

## Limitations
The policy only avoids collisions, with no navigation goal, and still crashes in the real world: a reflective glass door fooled it, and particularly novel situations confused it. The authors expect that finetuning on real data, or adding sensors such as depth cameras, would improve it.
