abstract: General-purpose robots coexisting with humans in their environment must learn to relate human language to their perceptions and actions to be useful in a range of daily tasks. Moreover, they need to acquire a diverse repertoire of general-purpose skills that allow composing long-horizon tasks by following unconstrained language instructions. In this paper, we present CALVIN (Composing Actions from Language and Vision), an open-source simulated benchmark to learn long-horizon language-conditioned tasks. Our aim is to make it possible to develop agents that can solve many robotic manipulation tasks over a long horizon, from onboard sensors, and specified only via human language. CALVIN tasks are more complex in terms of sequence length, action space, and language than existing vision-and-language task datasets and supports flexible specification of sensor suites. We evaluate the agents in zero-shot to novel language instructions and to novel environments and objects. We show that a baseline model based on multi-context imitation learning performs poorly on CALVIN, suggesting that there is significant room for developing innovative agents that learn to relate human language to their world models with this benchmark.

## What they do
CALVIN (Composing Actions from Language and Vision) is a simulated benchmark for long-horizon, language-conditioned manipulation. A single agent must carry out a series of free-form instructions in a row, such as "open the drawer ... pick up the blue block ... push the block into the drawer", with 7-DOF continuous control from onboard sensors. The authors also implement the MCIL baseline, which had no public implementation.

## How it works
- **Environments.** Four PyBullet environments (A-D) share a Franka Panda, a desk with a drawer, sliding door, button, switch and three colored blocks, but differ in textures and in where the static elements sit. 34 tasks are detected automatically from changes in simulator state.
- **Sensors and actions.** Static and gripper RGB-D cameras, a vision-based tactile sensor and proprioception; absolute or relative Cartesian actions or joint actions at 30 Hz.
- **Data.** About 24 hours of teleoperated play (about 2.4M steps) from three untrained operators. Only 1% is labeled with language, using 389 instructions (about 11 per task) applied procedurally, with MiniLM embeddings provided.
- **Evaluation.** Train on D, on all four, or zero-shot on A, B, C with test on D. MTLC runs 10 rollouts per task; LH-MTLC runs 1000 chains of five subtasks, unseen instructions and a neutral robot start.

## Results
- Static-camera MCIL trained and tested on D solves 53.9% of the 34 tasks. Adding the gripper camera, depth or tactile input leaves this about the same (46.1-54.2%).
- On five-instruction chains the same model completes the first subtask in 48.9% of chains, two in 12.9%, and all five in 0.08%. It sometimes confuses the red and blue blocks.
- Zero-shot (A, B, C to D), static-camera MTLC drops to 38.6%; 0.2% of chains get through two subtasks and none through three.

## Limitations
Objects are unicolored primitive shapes, chosen to keep the problem tractable, and everything runs in simulation. The only baseline is MCIL with absolute actions and no image augmentation. The authors note that its performance depends heavily on the robot's initial position.
