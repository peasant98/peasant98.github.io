abstract: 'Humanoid robots operating in cluttered environments must react to unexpected contacts before the first impact peak, which arrives within tens of milliseconds. Learned whole-body policies running at 50 Hz introduce a policy-cycle delay and may suppress a protective withdrawal as a tracking error. To address these challenges, we propose ARC (Ascending Reflex Control), a bottom-up framework that integrates instantaneous tactile reflexes with learned whole-body control. ARC consists of two components: an instantaneous reflex pathway and bottom-up reflex integration. The reflex pathway detects hazardous contacts from full-body tactile sensing, solves a whole-body withdrawal pose, and delivers it directly to the motors within 10 ms. Bottom-up reflex integration combines reflex-in-the-loop policy learning, reflex-transparent policy alignment, and reflex-informed policy adaptation to sustain the withdrawal, maintain balance, and complete longer-horizon recovery. We deploy ARC on a Unitree G1 and evaluate three categories of hazardous contact. Compared with an otherwise identical reflex delayed by 20 ms, ARC reduces the first force peak by 34.1% and reduces the contact impulse from 0.2632 N s to 0.1225 N s (a 2.15x ratio). ## What they do
Learned humanoid controllers run at 50 Hz, so a contact can wait up to 20 ms before the policy responds, and a tracking policy treats a protective withdrawal as an error to correct. ARC (Ascending Reflex Control) adds a tactile reflex that bypasses the policy and commands the motors directly, then trains the whole-body policy to keep the robot balanced and on task while the reflex acts on the same joints.

## How it works
- **Tactile startle detection.** Piezoresistive fabric patches (128 taxels each, 300 Hz) cover the wrists, elbows, knees, chest, back and feet. Per-taxel adaptive thresholds and connected-component grouping flag a hazardous contact on its first frame, within 5 ms of onset.
- **Instant reflex solver.** A constrained whole-body optimization picks a withdrawal pose that moves the struck point back along the surface normal, charges motion by the whole-body inertia, keeps the support frames fixed and avoids self-collision. A short sequence of local QPs solves it onboard in under 4 ms.
- **Reflex-aware policy.** A PPO motion-tracking policy is trained in Isaac Lab with reflexes injected. The reflex offset is subtracted from its observed joint positions and added back to its outputs, with DAgger-style supervision toward the reflex-free action, so it does not undo the reflex.
- **Recovery.** During a reflex, an AMP reward from human repositioning motions makes the policy walk away from the contact.

## Results
- Box strike on hardware: against the same reflex delayed by 20 ms, the first force peak drops from 21.64 N to 14.25 N (34.1%) and the impulse from 0.2632 to 0.1225 N s.
- Ablations: without reflex-in-the-loop training, survival falls to 82.0% and the robot re-hits the obstacle in 90.9% of trials. Without transparency the policy opposes the reflex and sustained withdrawal drops from 11.06 to 6.74 cm. Tracking an explicit retreat motion instead of AMP reaches 1.5 m in 14.1% of trials versus 63.0%.
- Demonstrated on more than ten tracking tasks in clutter, on teleoperation through the ScaleBFM general tracker without retraining, and on stepping over a threshold and climbing a step without vision, using the foot patches.

## Limitations
Patches cover only the convex regions that collide most often, so contacts elsewhere go unprotected. The hardware impact numbers come from one controlled box-strike setup. The tracking, teleoperation and traversal cases are reported as demonstrations, without success rates.
