abstract: 'People can anticipate how an object moves and the effort an interaction may require by watching others. How can embodied agents acquire this understanding from video? Force measurements provide direct sensory supervision, but require specialized equipment and remain limited in quantity. We introduce VidForce, which estimates an interaction trajectory together with a force profile from a third-person RGB video; 2D contact clicks and textual object-action prompts are optional. Our approach makes limited instrumented measurements useful for interpreting human interactions through learned physical prototypes as a prior. The prototypes share learned force levels and temporal profiles across interactions of comparable effort, with visual and semantic cues determining their contribution to each observation. Geometric and vision-language features adapt this prior to the observed interaction, while a joint conditional flow refines an initial tracked trajectory together with the force profile. Paired human and instrumented-gripper recordings provide measured force references for supervising human video, and a separately trained torque model demonstrates that the formulation extends to other sensory quantities. We evaluate trajectory recovery and force estimation through baseline comparisons and controlled ablations on held-out interactions from the Hoi! dataset. On this evaluation subset, VidForce achieves 6.6 cm ATE, peak-force MAE and RMSE of 4.81 N and 5.92 N, respectively, and force-profile RMSE of 5.52 N. On real-world videos, VidForce infers motion and force reliably and consistently despite the variability in human execution. We further deploy VidForce on a Spot robot, where trajectories and force profiles estimated from human demonstrations drive real-world articulated-object manipulation, and report success rates across repeated trials. ## What they do
Videos of people opening doors and drawers show how the object moves but not how hard to pull. From a third-person RGB clip, VidForce estimates a 3D contact trajectory and the force profile a robot gripper would need to apply; a vision-language model can supply the optional contact click and text prompt. Supervision comes from the Hoi! dataset, whose paired human and instrumented-gripper recordings give measured force references for bare-hand video.

## How it works
- **Frozen features.** D4RT tracks points into a motion field and an initial trajectory, DA3 estimates depth and intrinsics, and Qwen3-VL gives visual features and a prompt embedding. A fusion transformer combines them.
- **Force prototypes.** Ten learned prototypes each hold a force level, a temporal shape and a semantic key. A router, trained to favor prototypes near the measured peak, mixes them, so they organize by effort rather than object category. An adaptation head fits the mixed prior to the instance.
- **Joint flow.** A flow-matching transformer predicts residuals on the tracked trajectory and the force prior together.
- **Robot use.** On Spot, the trajectory is the reference of a Cartesian impedance controller and the force profile a feed-forward term along the path.

## Results
- On 99 held-out Hoi! sequences, median ATE is 6.6 cm versus 11.8 cm for the D4RT initialization, and peak-force MAE is 4.81 N versus 6.85 N for GPT-5.5.
- With force labels on only a quarter of the training episodes it still beats all baselines.
- Spot, five trials per condition and viewpoint: the oven door opened 7/10 with the predicted profile and 0/10 both without force and with a constant 20 N. The drawer opened 9/10 with the profile, 9/10 with a constant 8 N and 8/10 without force.
- In MuJoCo twins, the profile opens empty and loaded drawers with zero impact energy at the end stop; a constant at the peak force hits with 1.14 to 1.43 J.

## Limitations
It predicts typical effort from gripper references, not measured human force, and underestimates the heaviest interactions. Speed and contact differences between paired human and gripper videos are not modeled. It needs a static camera and a visible interaction, metric scale comes from monocular depth, and the robot trials cover one drawer and one oven door.
