abstract: Controlling the shape profile of soft trunk robots extends their reach into complex regions of the workspace and enhances their use in practical applications. Recent advances in soft robot design have enabled soft trunk robots to achieve greater flexibility in conforming to complex shapes. Specifically, trunk robots whose geometries are designed as helicoids have been shown to enable extreme shape-changing capabilities. Their compliance makes them highly nonlinear, which in turn makes shape control difficult. On similar tendon-driven trunk robots, model-based controllers based on the theory of adiabatic spectral submanifolds (aSSMs) have achieved high accuracy in closed-loop end-effector tracking. We extend this approach by learning an aSSM model of the full body of a trimmed-helicoid soft trunk robot from data and use this in a model predictive control (MPC) scheme to demonstrate a range of shape control tasks on hardware. On closed-loop dynamic shape regulation, our method outperforms the available linear baselines by a factor of 10. In human-in-the-loop interactive demos, our method completes section-by-section shaping and balances a tennis ball for over 25 seconds, while the linear baselines fall short. A model-free reinforcement learning policy trained for 12 hours on hardware fails on the same balancing task. These results establish aSSM-based MPC as a practical route to full-body shape control on real soft hardware.

## What they do
Soft trunk robots meant for confined spaces need control over the shape of their whole body, not just the tip, but their compliance makes the shape dynamics strongly nonlinear. The authors learn an adiabatic spectral submanifold (aSSM) model of the full trunk from hardware data. Earlier aSSM models were used only for end-effector tracking. Here, MPC on the model controls the poses of three points along a 500 mm helicoid trunk.

## How it works
- **Hardware.** Five 3D-printed TPU helicoid segments, nine tendons driving three independent sections, and motion-capture markers at the end of every segment. Nesting each tendon's PTFE sleeve in an extension spring cuts the uncommanded bending of lower sections from up to 58° to 6°.
- **Model.** About 5 h of responses to step inputs (20,000 static commands from a Sobol sequence) give a nonlinear map from motor commands to rest shape. Around that map they fit a 6-dimensional polynomial model of the dynamics.
- **Control.** A sampling-based MPC scores three rounds of 1000 candidate inputs against the model and runs at 20 Hz. The baselines run the same MPC on three linear models (ERA, DMDc, LOpInf) fit to the same data.

## Results
- Open loop over 750 steps, the mean tip error is 27.5 mm, 2.9x lower than ERA, the best linear model.
- Shape regulation: the aSSM reaches all 8 target shapes in 11.8 s on average with 5.1 mm mean error; no linear baseline reaches any target, and the best of them ends at 56.1 mm.
- Tracking a 30 s moving shape: 5.6 mm mean position error and 2.6° orientation error, against 30.8 mm and 15.1° for ERA.
- Following a hand-held wand, it balances a tennis ball on the tip for over 25 s. ERA and DMDc lose the ball after 2 s and 0.4 s. A PPO policy trained for 12 h directly on the robot drops it after 1.8 s.

## Limitations
Tendon speed limits how fast the shape can change. Shape feedback comes from an eight-camera motion-capture system with markers on every segment. The authors frame the RL comparison as a sample-efficiency result under a 12 h hardware budget, not a head-to-head comparison of methods.
