abstract: In vision-based teleoperation of robotic hands, the operator receives no haptic feedback and therefore struggles to regulate the grasp force, while adding tactile sensors to the fingers increases wiring, cost and integration complexity. Compliant hands offer an alternative, as their mechanical compliance, usually treated only as a passive property for safe and adaptive grasping, can also serve as a source of information. Here, we show that the elastic transmission of a compliant, tendon-driven anthropomorphic hand can itself act as the sensor for shared-control telemanipulation. Series springs in the tendon routing are read by potentiometers in a forearm-mounted motor box, from which the hand estimates its joint states and finger forces without any external sensors. Building on this self-sensing, we develop a contact-aware shared-control framework for VR-based teleoperation. The robot hand is controlled through a mapping from the operator's hand kinematics until a contact is detected proprioceptively. Control then switches to a finger-level force controller whose set point is determined by the operator's grasping speed, and control authority can be allocated to individual fingers. Experiments validate the system's force estimation and increased grasp success rate compared with direct teleoperation for fragile and heavy objects. These results show that compliance can serve not only for passive adaptation but also as an intrinsic sensing resource for physical human-robot collaboration.

## What they do
In VR hand teleoperation the operator sees the scene but cannot feel it, so fragile objects get crushed and heavy ones slip. Instead of adding tactile sensors to the fingers, this work uses the springs already in a compliant tendon-driven hand to sense contact. The hand takes over the grasp at contact, and the operator sets the grip force by how fast they close their hand.

## How it works
- **Self-sensing.** The 21-DoF hand is driven by 15 servos in a forearm motor box. A spring sits in series with each finger's MCP tendon and with both thumb CMC tendons, and linear potentiometers read its elongation. A kernel SVR maps motor positions and spring signal to joint angles. A linear model with a friction threshold maps spring change to fingertip force during loading.
- **Contact and speed.** An adaptive baseline flags contact. The spring's loading rate, measured over at most 350 ms after onset, classifies the closure as slow or fast.
- **Regulation.** After contact the operator's further closing is ignored, and each finger servos its spring to a target: a gentle level for slow closures and a higher one for fast closures. Per-finger state machines let the ulnar fingers hold one object while the operator keeps control of the thumb and index finger.

## Results
- Joint angles are estimated to about 3 deg RMSE (R² ≥ 0.98). On held-out ramps up to about 0.8 N, five of six channels estimate fingertip force with 18 mN mean RMSE. The spring explains 94% of the force variance, while the servo current explains 23%.
- Across eight samples from rigid plastic to the softest Ecoflex, one threshold classified 95% of 240 contacts correctly.
- With the matching speed, success rose from 35% to 80% for the cup and from 45% to 90% for the bottle.

## Limitations
There are only two force levels, and the thresholds were tuned empirically. The force estimate saturates under heavy loads and depends on which phalanx is loaded. One spring per finger cannot localize contact or detect slip. Only two members of the research team teleoperated the hand.
