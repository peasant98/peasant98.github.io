abstract: Robots operating in previously mapped spaces must localize accurately despite moving people and rearranged objects. Most localization pipelines match the query against a sparse set of captured reference images retrieved by appearance, which is brittle under scene change and repeating structures. We present MERLoc, which turns a single metric 3D Gaussian Splatting scan into a dense Functional Reference Memory. Reference views are sampled over the navigable space for the robot's morphology and sensors at a target density and rendered offline. Pre-encoding them for the chosen off-the-shelf localizer removes the reference encoder from inference. A pose-based retrieval strategy then selects references by spatial proximity and viewing direction rather than appearance, sidestepping visual ambiguities whenever a prior exists. Across one classical and four learned localizers in simulated and real dynamic scenes, pose-based retrieval matches or outperforms appearance retrieval in most cases, even when the latter is gated by the same prior. On real data, it lowers position RMSE by 15-60 % in nine of ten backend/split combinations, and the rendered memory outperforms real-image caches posed by Structure-from-Motion. The system runs onboard a Jetson Orin NX at 5 Hz, enabling a navigation policy to successfully traverse a hallway with people roaming and rearranging objects.

## What they do
Visual localization in a mapped space usually retrieves a few captured reference images by appearance and hands them to a relative pose solver. Repeated structures and rearranged scenes make that retrieval return wrong references, which no solver recovers from. MERLoc replaces the captured images with views rendered from a 3D Gaussian Splatting scan, chooses them by pose, and runs off-the-shelf localizers without per-scene training.

## How it works
- **Reference memory.** From the scan's collision mesh they extract the navigable floor and sample camera poses within a height band and orientation range matched to the robot's camera, at a set density (1000 per m³ by default). Each view is rendered with color and depth and encoded offline for the chosen localizer, so no reference is encoded at runtime.
- **Pose-based retrieval.** With the previous estimate or odometry as a prior, it gathers references within 1.5 m, keeps the 64 best aligned in viewing direction, and takes the K nearest. Appearance retrieval (NetVLAD) is used only to initialize or after five failures in a row.
- **Localizers.** A classical pipeline (ALIKED features, LightGlue, PnP) and four feed-forward models (MonST3R, Reloc3r, VGGT-Ω, π³) with public weights; known reference poses and rendered depth make their outputs metric.

## Results
- In simulated scenes with rearranged objects, pose-based retrieval beats appearance retrieval for four of five localizers. With 4 to 24 simulated people, four localizers lose at most seven recall points.
- Real motion-capture scene with a bipedal robot, scanned after the objects were moved: the classical localizer reaches 0.864 recall at 5 cm/5° and 8.2 cm RMSE on the dynamic sequence, against 0.830 and 17.9 cm with appearance retrieval, and at best 0.636 and 16.6 cm with real images of the same scene posed by Structure-from-Motion.
- The classical localizer with K=4 runs at 5 Hz on a Jetson Orin NX. Driven by a navigation policy in a hallway where people moved around and rearranged objects, the robot reached every goal in two runs of four and three goals spaced 30 m apart.

## Limitations
The real benchmark is a 14.2 m² scene with two people, which the authors say offers little aliasing. Pose-based retrieval needs about 100 references per m³ or more to beat appearance retrieval; at 1000 per m³ with a 1 m height band the memory takes 120 MB per m² of floor. VGGT-Ω and π³ gain little or lose recall, only the classical localizer reaches 5 Hz onboard, and the hallway test is qualitative and uses odometry priors.
