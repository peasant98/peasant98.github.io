abstract: Simulations are attractive environments for training agents as they provide an abundant source of data and alleviate certain safety concerns during the training process. But the behaviours developed by agents in simulation are often specific to the characteristics of the simulator. Due to modeling error, strategies that are successful in simulation may not transfer to their real world counterparts. In this paper, we demonstrate a simple method to bridge this "reality gap". By randomizing the dynamics of the simulator during training, we are able to develop policies that are capable of adapting to very different dynamics, including ones that differ significantly from the dynamics on which the policies were trained. This adaptivity enables the policies to generalize to the dynamics of the real world without any training on the physical system. Our approach is demonstrated on an object pushing task using a robotic arm. Despite being trained exclusively in simulation, our policies are able to maintain a similar level of performance when deployed on a real robot, reliably moving an object to a desired location from random initial configurations. We explore the impact of various design decisions and show that the resulting policies are robust to significant calibration error.

## What they do
Policies trained in simulation tend to exploit the simulator's quirks and fail on the real robot. Instead of calibrating the simulator carefully, the authors randomize its dynamics during training and give the policy memory, so it can infer the dynamics it is facing at run time. They test this on pushing a puck to a random target with a 7-DoF Fetch arm, trained only in MuJoCo with a sparse binary reward.

## How it works
- **Dynamics randomization.** 95 parameters are randomized in total. Link masses, joint damping, puck mass, friction and damping, table height and controller gains are sampled once per episode. The time between actions (a simple latency model) and Gaussian observation noise change every step.
- **Recurrent policy.** An LSTM branch reads the current state and previous action to implicitly identify the dynamics. A feedforward branch handles the goal. Actions are relative joint-angle targets for a position controller.
- **Training.** Recurrent DDPG with Hindsight Experience Replay. The critic also sees the true dynamics parameters, which the policy never gets. Training takes about 100 million samples, roughly 8 hours on a 100-core cluster.

## Results
- Real-robot success: LSTM 0.89 ± 0.06 (28 trials), feedforward with an 8-step history 0.70, plain feedforward 0.67, feedforward without randomization 0.0. In simulation the LSTM scores 0.91.
- Ablations on the real robot: a fixed action timestep drops success to 0.29, no observation noise to 0.25, fixed puck friction to 0.48 and fixed link mass to 0.64.
- With a bag of chips taped under the puck to change its contact dynamics, the LSTM still succeeds 0.91 ± 0.04.

## Limitations
Only one task, puck pushing, was tested. The policy works from full state, with the puck tracked by a PhaseSpace mocap system rather than vision. Real-robot evaluations use 10 to 28 trials per model. The authors leave other tasks and vision inputs to future work.
