abstract: We present HoloBrain-0.5, a family of vision-language-action (VLA) models for manipulation across robot embodiments. The policies plan in a shared action space using robot state, kinematic structure, and calibrated camera observations. We standardize joint axes, coordinate frames, and tool-center points in robot descriptions (URDFs) and encode their kinematic graphs in a shared 3D representation. HoloBrain-0.5-Continuous generates action chunks by diffusion, while HoloBrain-0.5-Discrete uses residual finite scalar quantization (FSQ) and delayed autoregression. We pretrain both policies on over 10,000 hours of real-robot, simulation, and human-manipulation data spanning 15 embodiment categories. Across six closed-loop simulation settings, the discrete policy converges faster and reaches a higher mean success rate than the continuous policy (56.77% vs. 49.71%). On seven real-robot tasks, it achieves a mean progress score of 87.76% without task-specific post-training. After post-training, the continuous policy outperforms $π_{0.5}$ on the seven-task benchmark (94.52% vs. 86.25%) and an 11-task dual-arm benchmark (79.07% vs. 69.41%). The discrete-initialized continuous policy transfers five tasks zero-shot from Piper to Piper-X, scoring 63.28% on average. With target-robot demonstrations, the continuous policy scores 89.2% on Franka Panda and 92.94% on a mobile dual-arm platform. Although pretraining includes no Unitree G1 data, post-training with limited G1 demonstrations yields a mean progress score of 70.24% across three humanoid tasks, compared with 11.00% for $π_{0.5}$.

## What they do
HoloBrain-0.5 extends the authors' HoloBrain-0 vision-language-action model so that one policy can control robots with different joint counts and kinematics. Besides images and an instruction, the policy receives the robot's URDF and camera calibration, and it predicts actions on the robot's kinematic graph. Two variants are released, one generating actions by diffusion and one predicting discrete action codes. Both are pretrained on 10,006.5 hours of real-robot (5,704.3 h), simulation (3,470.3 h) and human-hand (831.9 h) data.

## How it works
- **URDF standardization.** Every actuated joint is re-expressed to rotate about, or slide along, its local +z axis, and every tool-center point faces outward along +z. Compensating transforms leave the physical geometry and recorded trajectories unchanged.
- **Continuous variant.** A Qwen3.5-2B backbone feeds a diffusion decoder whose image, text and joint-temporal attention branches run in parallel and are mixed by a learned router. In HoloBrain-0 these branches ran in sequence, which made the policy over-rely on proprioception.
- **Discrete variant.** A tokenizer places "virtual joints" on the kinematic graph and compresses each 64-frame chunk with 3-stage residual FSQ. The policy then predicts the code grid diagonal by diagonal, 192 codes in 21 rounds.
- **Calibration.** Open datasets that lack camera calibration get it from hand-annotated landmarks refined by bundle adjustment.

## Results
- After pretraining, mean success over six simulation settings is 56.77% for the discrete policy and 49.71% for the continuous one.
- Seven real-robot tasks: the discrete policy reaches 87.76% progress without task-specific training. After post-training on 200 demos per task, the continuous policy reaches 94.52% (π0.5: 86.25%). An 11-task dual-arm benchmark gives 79.07% versus 69.41%.
- Zero-shot transfer of five tasks from Piper to Piper-X: 63.28% versus 17.44% for π0.5.
- With target-robot data: Franka 89.2% vs. 85.0%, mobile dual-arm 92.94% vs. 73.83%, Unitree G1 70.24% vs. 11.00%.

## Limitations
Post-training with few demonstrations hurts the discrete policy: its average falls from 87.76% to 69.74%. The policy acts on the current observation only, with no memory, and uses no touch or force sensing. Real-robot results are milestone-based progress scores, not binary success, and some tasks remain weak (handing a bag to a person on the G1: 27.73%).
