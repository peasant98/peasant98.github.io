abstract: Imitation learning policies in robotics are trained on datasets collected through costly real-world teleoperation. Often, the data-collection process is unguided, relying on the teleoperator's intuition, resulting in data that contributes little to overall policy performance. Recent work has shown that curating such unguided datasets can substantially improve policy performance, but these approaches operate by filtering existing datasets after collection and therefore do not prevent the acquisition of uninformative data. In this work, we propose GUILD, a novel Guided Data Collection (GDC) method that actively reasons about which data should be collected to maximize downstream policy performance. We introduce an iterative framework that alternates between 1) task proposal and data collection and 2) data evaluation to close the loop. For task proposal, we leverage semantic priors from vision-language models to identify relevant environments, object configurations, and goals for data collection. However, since semantic relevance does not imply policy improvement, we perform data evaluation. By leveraging predictive data attribution we approximate how the collected trajectories influence policy performance on a target deployment setting. This signal is then used as feedback to guide subsequent data collection, closing the data collection loop. We evaluate our approach in simulation and real robot settings, demonstrating that guided data collection substantially improves sample efficiency and deployment performance compared to passive data collection strategies, multiple baselines and experienced roboticists. More information at https://robin-lab.cs.utexas.edu/guild2026/

## What they do
Robots are often deployed where collecting demonstrations is hard (an end-user home), while teleoperation is easy in other labs or mock setups. GUILD treats data collection itself as the optimization problem: given a few target demos and a fixed budget, it decides which tasks to collect in the accessible environments, instead of collecting broadly and filtering afterwards.

## How it works
- **Task proposal.** GPT-5.2 sees a video and description of the target task plus the available scenes and objects, and proposes tasks: scene, object configuration and a language instruction. Every few rounds it proposes more, given a utility-ranked list of earlier tasks.
- **Data evaluation.** An Octo-Small policy is co-trained 50/50 with the target demos. Metagradients (MAGIC, REPLAY) give each trajectory's effect on validation loss over the target demos; scores are aggregated into a per-task marginal utility.
- **Iterative collection.** Each round, a teleoperator collects a small batch (6 demos in the real world) from the highest-utility task, then utilities are recomputed. Real-world runs use 20 rounds.

## Results
- LIBERO (3 target tasks, data from 16 other scenes): GUILD matches or exceeds the strongest baseline at every budget, reaching 64.9% average success. Eight roboticists given the same budget average below GUILD.
- Real world (6 rooms, 4 target tasks, 20 rollouts each): selecting from a fixed 49-task dataset, GUILD beats all baselines and an All-Data policy trained on everything, including target-room data.
- Open-ended real-world collection: about 25% higher average success than VLM-Uniform, and higher on average than two experts with 4-5 years of experience.
- One attribution step takes 10-15 min on four A5000 GPUs; a full real-world run takes 10-15 h.

## Limitations
The VLM can propose infeasible or unoriginal tasks, and swapping it changes per-task results. Smaller batches give better utility estimates but need more attribution passes. Tasks are discretized by the VLM proposals, and a few target-domain demos are still required.
