Our system is trained in simulation, where it has access to ground-truth object shapes and physical properties. Then we distill it to operate on realistic yet noisy simulated visuotactile and proprioceptive sensory inputs. ## What they do
Earlier sim-to-real rotation policies on the Allegro hand, such as Hora, turned objects only about the z-axis. RotateIt rotates objects continuously about the x, y or z axis using only the fingertips, with no supporting surface. It is trained entirely in simulation and deployed on the real hand without fine-tuning.

## How it works
- **Oracle.** PPO in IsaacGym on hundreds of objects. The policy sees proprioception plus privileged information: a PointNet encoding of the object's point cloud, and its pose and physical properties. The reward pays for spin about the target axis and penalizes spin about the other axes.
- **Visuotactile student.** A transformer reads a history of segmented object depth, fingertip contact locations, joint positions and actions, and regresses the oracle's privileged encoding.
- **Sim-to-real.** Touch is reduced to one of 8 contact directions per fingertip, read on the real hand by tracking the highest-intensity pixel of a vision-based touch sensor. Real depth is masked with Segment Anything.
- One policy is trained per axis; a distilled multi-axis policy performs on par (105 vs 110 reward on +x).

## Results
- Adding object shape lifts the oracle's x-axis rotation reward from 85 to 125 (Hora: 79).
- The student with vision and touch scores 118 / 109 / 136 on x / y / z, versus 79 / 67 / 108 with proprioception only. Contact location helps (102 vs 79 on x without vision); binary contact does not (80).
- On 15 held-out harder objects, reward drops 41% with proprioception only and 15% with vision and touch.
- Real hand, x-axis: Hora cannot finish the task and the object falls; RotateIt turns cubes, spheres and cylinders about 2π radians within 20 s, including objects outside the training set.

## Limitations
Objects cannot be long (a pencil or screwdriver) and must fit the hand's mechanical limits. The policy is frozen after training, so it cannot learn from real-world experience, and touch uses only the contact location rather than the full tactile image.
