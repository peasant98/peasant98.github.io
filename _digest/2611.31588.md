abstract: 'Sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) has enabled impressive robotic behaviors, yet deploying policies directly from vision remains challenging. A common approach first trains a teacher policy with RL using privileged state observations, then distills its behavior into a vision-based student via imitation. However, the resulting student can substantially underperform its teacher. Directly training visual policies with RL offers a promising alternative, but learning task-relevant visual representations from scratch can be sample-inefficient, making one-phase visual RL intractable. We propose DRIFT, a simple recipe for efficient one-phase visual RL using image observations available at test time. Our key insight is to use frozen, pretrained foundation vision encoders, allowing RL to focus on learning control from existing visual representations. Freezing the encoder avoids costly backpropagation through the vision backbone, while its pretrained representations enable real-world transfer from fast, rasterized simulation rendering. Under matched architectures, observations, and compute budgets, DRIFT outperforms two-phase distillation and achieves a 58.8% relative improvement in average real-world success rate across three manipulation tasks. Controlled simulation evaluations demonstrate that DRIFT''s relative advantage grows from roughly 9% in-distribution to over 61% under out-of-distribution shifts, suggesting that stronger generalization contributes to superior real-world transfer. ## What they do
The usual way to get a vision-based sim-to-real policy has two phases. First a teacher is trained with RL on privileged simulator state, then it is distilled into an image-based student, which can fall well short of the teacher. Training the image policy with RL directly is usually too slow because the encoder has to learn from rewards. DRIFT makes it practical by freezing a pretrained encoder. The paper compares the two routes with matched architectures, observations and compute.

## How it works
- **Frozen encoder.** DINOv3 ViT-S/16 features from a wrist camera. There is no backpropagation through the backbone, and the features are computed once and reused across PPO epochs, so far more experience fits into an hour of training.
- **Policy.** An attention-pooling head compresses the patch features into a few visual tokens. A small three-layer transformer combines them with proprioception and outputs joint-position deltas and a gripper command.
- **Training.** PPO in ManiSkill, with a critic that sees privileged state. Rendering is fast rasterization rather than ray tracing. Textures, lighting and camera pose are randomized, and about 27k natural images serve as backgrounds. Camera calibration and actuator system identification match the simulation to the real arm.

## Results
- Zero-shot on a Franka Research 3: peg insertion (2 mm clearance) 66.7% vs 23.3% for distillation with the same frozen encoder, and Push-T 76.7% vs 30.0%, although simulation success differed by only a few points. Distilling with an unfrozen encoder transferred worse still (6.7%, 16.7%).
- Pick-Cube: both methods reach 100%, but on four unseen objects DRIFT averages 88.8% vs 50.0%.
- In simulation the gap is 8.6% (relative) in-distribution. Under shifts in mass, scale, initial state, viewpoint and force, distillation loses about 42% of its success and DRIFT 14%.
- Encoder choice matters: DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B and SigLIP2 ViT-B/16 reach about 73% in simulation but only 3.3% and 10.0% on the real robot.

## Limitations
The paper has no limitations section. The tests cover three short tabletop tasks on one arm with one wrist camera. Five real-world rollouts per checkpoint were used to select checkpoints, and simulation success alone did not predict which encoder would transfer.
