abstract: Prosthetic bypasses allow able-bodied individuals to walk with a prosthesis, enabling safe early-stage testing of new prosthetic devices before clinical trials. However, existing transtibial bypasses impose constraints, such as a contralateral shoe lift or a lateral offset of the prosthesis, and their effects on gait remain poorly documented. We present an open-source transtibial prosthetic bypass that preserves part of the sagittal knee mobility without these constraints. Six able-bodied participants familiarized themselves with the bypass for approximately 10 min, then walked on a treadmill with and without it, and joint kinematics were recorded with motion capture and then extracted with OpenSim. All participants were able to walk without handrail support, although single stance remained shorter on the bypass side (27% vs. 45% of the gait cycle). Bypass-side ankle kinematics were consistent with those reported for transtibial prosthesis users, whereas peak knee flexion during swing decreased from 61° to 36° (p<0.001) and hip range of the sound leg increased from 35° to 51° (p<0.01). Although gait with the bypass does not fully replicate that of prosthesis users, it yielded consistent gait patterns after only 10 min of learning. This bypass therefore provides a useful tool for the lower-limb prosthetics research community, facilitating preliminary experiments with able-bodied participants.

## What they do
A prosthetic bypass lets an able-bodied person walk on a prosthesis, so new prosthetic devices can be tested before trials with amputees. Existing transtibial bypasses either mount the prosthesis under the wearer's foot, which needs a shoe lift on the other leg, or beside the leg, which offsets it laterally. The authors release a bypass that avoids both and keeps part of the knee's sagittal motion. They then measure how six first-time users learn to walk with it and how it changes their gait.

## How it works
- **Hardware.** A commercial knee orthosis holds the knee flexed. A 3D-printed adapter connects it to a translation module and an adjustable tube (±14° angle, 6 cm height) ending in a standard pyramid, so any pyramid-mounted foot fits. It weighs 2.2 kg without a foot. Parts, STEP files and assembly instructions are released under GPL.
- **Learning protocol.** Five phases: load awareness with visual feedback, rebalancing, single-leg stance on the bypass, first steps in a corridor, then 15 s treadmill walks at 3 km/h ending in an unassisted walk.
- **Evaluation.** OptiTrack motion capture with OpenSim inverse kinematics, plus pressure insoles for gait events. Baseline walking is compared with walking on the Össur Balance and Pro-Flex feet.

## Results
- Learning took 9.4 ± 2.2 min. Bypass-side single support rose from 19 ± 15% to 27 ± 2% of the gait cycle (baseline 34%), while the sound side stayed longer at 45% (baseline 35%).
- Bypass-side ankle range of motion (14°) and velocity range (152°/s) match published data from Flex-Foot users (14°, 150°/s).
- Swing knee flexion on the bypass side fell from 61° to 36° (p<0.001) and knee range nearly halved (59° to 32°). Sound-side hip range rose from 35° to 51° (p=0.004).
- Participants rated comfort 6.5/10 and ease of learning 6.3/10.

## Limitations
Six participants, a 10-minute familiarization that cannot separate device effects from inexperience, and a fixed treadmill speed. A prosthetist did not fit the bypass. The flexed knee rules out sitting or high steps, the orthosis restricts non-sagittal rotation, and the prosthetic knee allows about 52° of non-physiological extension. The analysis covers sagittal kinematics in level walking only.
