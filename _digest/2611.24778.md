abstract: 'World models operate in a world they cannot fully observe. When a hidden disturbance, such as a current pushing a boat, changes the outcome of an action, a counterfactual question follows: what would have happened under a different action, subject to the same disturbance? Its answer separates bad decisions from bad luck. We prove that world models trained on factual transitions cannot answer it on their own, whatever their data or capacity. For many physical systems, the observed outcome and a simple property of the dynamics suffice: natura non facit saltus, nature makes no leaps. When actions and disturbances act as separate forces, their interaction is second-order in time, so the counterfactual outcome is, to first order, the observed outcome plus the difference between the world model''s predictions under the alternative and factual actions. This yields a counterfactual world model with no counterfactual data, extra parameters, or retraining. For transitions of duration $h$, we prove an $O(h^2)$ bound on the counterfactual error relative to the disturbance variance, establish a matching minimax lower bound from unpaired factual data, and characterize failures at discontinuities, coordinate folds, and disturbances that scale the command. We validate these predictions from pixels with JEPA world models on frozen DINOv2 and masked autoencoder (MAE) encoders. On four rendered simulators, our approach improves counterfactual $R^2$ from $38$--$46\%$ to $86$--$96\%$ with the same world models; on a fifth, an underwater vehicle, it reaches $90$--$94\%$ with sixteen times more factual data. Four further frozen encoders, DINOv3, SigLIP~2, CLIP and EVA-02, give $81$--$95\%$ on the same simulators. In hindsight planning, it finds the action that would have reached the goal in $35$--$69\%$ of events, against $14$--$23\%$ for an oracle planner that knows the simulator but not the outcome.'

## What they do
A world model predicts where an action leads on average over hidden disturbances, such as a current pushing a boat. Once an outcome is observed, the question becomes where another action would have led under the same disturbance. The authors prove a world model trained on factual transitions cannot answer this alone, and show that for many physical systems a simple correction can.

## How it works
- **Residual reuse.** The counterfactual outcome is the observed outcome plus the world model's predicted difference between the alternative and the actual action: two predictor calls, no counterfactual data, extra parameters or retraining.
- **Why it holds.** When action and disturbance enter as separate forces, their interaction is second-order in the transition duration h, so the error is O(h²); a matching minimax lower bound shows this rate is the best possible from unpaired factual data.
- **Setup.** JEPA predictors on seven frozen image encoders (DINOv2, MAE and others), trained on factual transitions in rendered simulators such as HandReach, FetchReach and the BlueROV2 underwater vehicle.

## Results
- On HandReach, Ball-in-Cup, FetchReach and PassWater, counterfactual R² rises from 38.1-45.6% for the same predictors to 85.6-95.7%, above the 49.1-51.1% of an oracle that knows the simulator state but not the outcome. Correcting the image features directly, without state labels, gives 73.4-94.1%.
- BlueROV2 needs more data: 90.1-93.5% with 16x the factual episodes.
- Hindsight planning succeeds in 35-69% of events, against 9-25% for the trained predictor and 14-23% for the oracle planner.
- The predicted failures appear: with Push-T contacts and full gain faults, the correction does worse than ignoring the outcome.

## Limitations
All environments are simulated, with controlled actuator faults. The rule needs coordinates in which action and disturbance add (raw pixels and cos(pitch) do not), fails with contacts and command-scaling faults, and covers one transition at a time. Whether it is safe on a system can only be checked with paired outcomes, not factual logs.
