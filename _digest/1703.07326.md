abstract: 'Imitation learning has been commonly applied to solve different tasks in isolation. This usually requires either careful feature engineering, or a significant number of samples. This is far from what we desire: ideally, robots should be able to learn from very few demonstrations of any given task, and instantly generalize to new situations of the same task, without requiring task-specific engineering. In this paper, we propose a meta-learning framework for achieving such capability, which we call one-shot imitation learning. Specifically, we consider the setting where there is a very large set of tasks, and each task has many instantiations. For example, a task could be to stack all blocks on a table into a single tower, another task could be to place all blocks on a table into two-block towers, etc. In each case, different instances of the task would consist of different sets of blocks with different initial states. At training time, our algorithm is presented with pairs of demonstrations for a subset of all tasks. A neural net is trained that takes as input one demonstration and the current state (which initially is the initial state of the other demonstration of the pair), and outputs an action with the goal that the resulting sequence of states and actions matches as closely as possible with the second demonstration. At test time, a demonstration of a single instance of a new task is presented, and the neural net is expected to perform well on new instances of this new task. The use of soft attention allows the model to generalize to conditions and tasks unseen in the training data. We anticipate that by training this model on a much greater variety of tasks and settings, we will obtain a general system that can turn any demonstrations into robust policies that can accomplish an overwhelming variety of tasks. ## What they do
Instead of one policy per task, this paper trains a single network that takes one demonstration of a block-stacking task plus the current state and outputs an action. A demonstration of a new layout (e.g. `ab cd ef gh`: A on B, C on D, and so on) is then enough to build it from new block positions. Training is supervised learning on pairs of demonstrations of the same task.

## How it works
- **Setup.** A 7-DoF Fetch arm stacks 2-10 blocks. Observations are block (x, y, z) positions relative to the gripper plus gripper state. There are 140 training tasks and 43 held-out test tasks, with 1000 noisy scripted demonstrations per task, trained with behavioral cloning or DAgger.
- **Demonstration network.** Temporal dropout discards 95% of the demonstration's timesteps. Dilated temporal convolutions and neighborhood attention, in which each block queries the other blocks relative to itself, embed the rest.
- **Context network.** A query from the current state attends over demonstration timesteps, then over the blocks in the current state. The result is a fixed-size embedding, whatever the demonstration length or block count.
- **Manipulation network.** An MLP maps that embedding to the action. At test time, 10 downsampled demonstrations are ensembled.

## Results
- On unseen test tasks, full-demonstration DAgger succeeds 94.9% of the time at 2 stages, 65.9% at 5 and 50.6% at 6. The scripted demonstrator gets 95.8%, 65.9% and 49.4%. Conditioning only on the final state falls to 35.9% at 6 stages.
- Behavioral cloning roughly matches DAgger (46.5% vs 50.6% on 6-stage test tasks), given noise injected into the demonstrations.
- Trained only on `abcd`, the policy scores 82-95% on all 24 block permutations of it.

## Limitations
Inputs are block positions, not images, and one demonstration must fully specify the task. Success falls as tasks get longer (18.0% on 8-stage test tasks), and most failures are irrecoverable manipulation errors, not misread demonstrations. Conditioning on full demonstrations trains more slowly than conditioning on final states or snapshots. No physical-robot experiments are reported.
