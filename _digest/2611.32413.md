abstract: 'Facial robots require many coordinated, small-amplitude motions, but accommodating these motions in a compact face remains difficult. This paper presents an anatomically inspired facial-robot platform that reproduces the layered organization of the human facial musculoskeletal system. The platform comprises bone, muscle, and skin components whose geometry, shape, and attachment locations are derived from three-dimensional anatomical data. Wire-driven, compliant muscle components are routed through PTFE tubes, allowing actuation to be placed away from the face. We describe the selection and fabrication of 65 muscles (34 muscle types) required to reproduce the 47 target action units (AUs) in the Facial Action Coding System (FACS). We then evaluate the reproducibility of individual AUs in two stages: muscle actuation without skin and actuation after the silicone skin is attached. Forty of the 47 target AUs were reproduced with the muscle components, and 30 were reproduced after skin attachment. These results demonstrate the feasibility of reproducing a broad range of FACS facial expressions using an anatomically inspired musculoskeletal structure. The proposed platform provides a foundation for the systematic development and evaluation of future anatomically inspired facial robots.'

## What they do
Most expressive robot faces move the skin directly with linkages and servos, and fitting those into a face-sized volume limits how many motions they can make. This platform instead copies the layers of a human face: bones, muscles and skin, with shapes and attachment points taken from the BodyParts3D anatomical data. The target is the 47 principal action units (AUs) of the Facial Action Coding System (FACS). Covering them takes 65 muscles of 34 types.

## How it works
- **Bones.** Five 3D-printed assemblies (head, jaw, neck, shoulder, tongue), mostly PLA with TPU for soft parts such as the gums. Muscles alone link the jaw, tongue and head, and muscles also move the eyeballs.
- **Muscles.** Each TPU muscle has a fixing part bolted to bone and a contracting part: a thin zigzag plate along the wire path, shaped to contract as far as possible. Python scripts in Rhino generate both parts from the anatomical muscle shape, with parameters that trade stiffness against contraction.
- **Actuation and skin.** Wires run through PTFE tubes, so the actuation can sit away from the face. Cast silicone skin is taped to the bones and tied to the muscle wires with small glued connectors.

## Results
- Each AU counts as reproduced if the intended motion is clearly visible while its wire is pulled by hand.
- Muscles without skin: 40 of 47 AUs, including separate inner and outer brow raising, sideways jaw motion, eye movements and showing the tongue.
- With skin: 30 of those 40. Some produce typical skin features, such as the chin bulge of AU17, a nasolabial fold in AU10 and wrinkles in AU9 and AU12.
- The 7 muscle-stage failures need air pressure (cheek puffing), better nostril attachment data or inward lip motion. The 10 lost with the skin come from weak eyelid-to-skin coupling, a tongue too short to protrude, wire tubes interfering with the jaw, and neck skin outside the platform.

## Limitations
No motors yet: every wire was pulled manually. The evaluation is qualitative and covers one AU at a time. There are no measurements of contraction or skin displacement, no comparison with human faces and no combined expressions; the authors list all of these as future work.
