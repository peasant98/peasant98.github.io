abstract: 'Human-in-the-loop optimization (HILO) is a common approach for optimizing the control of assistive devices to account for the wearer''s unique biomechanics and subjective preferences. However, despite research suggesting that a person may have a different prioritization of objectives depending on time-varying factors such as the environment, their mood, or energy levels, existing HILO approaches only consider a single objective or enforce a fixed weighting on a set of objectives. Neither approach is capable of representing an individual''s preferences over objectives. In this work, we propose Multi-Objective Human-in-the-loop Bayesian Optimization (MO-HILBO), which builds on explicit multi-objective Bayesian optimization to efficiently infer a personalized set of Pareto-optimal controllers. We compare our approach with an existing multi-objective HILO method and experimentally demonstrate MO-HILBO on a lower-limb exoskeleton across two objectives: metabolic cost (efficiency) and ordinal human feedback (comfort). We find that MO-HILBO (1) discovers Pareto-optimal controllers, and (2) that the pairwise ordering of points on the Pareto front itself is consistent with validation trials. Lastly, we open-source mohilo, a Python package for running both HILO and MO-HILBO on wearable devices: https://dynamicmobility.github.io/mohilo/.'

## What they do
Human-in-the-loop optimization usually tunes an exoskeleton for one objective, or for a fixed weighted sum of several. MO-HILBO instead learns each wearer's Pareto set: the controllers where neither comfort nor metabolic cost can improve without worsening the other. The authors compare it with an evolutionary baseline (NSGA-II) in simulation, run it on a hip/knee exoskeleton with three participants, and release it as the Python package mohilo.

## How it works
- **Surrogate.** One Gaussian process per objective, with hyperparameters fit under a prior on measurement noise. The Pareto set is read off the GP means.
- **Acquisition.** BoTorch's qLogNParEGO draws a new random weighting of the objectives at every query, so successive trials probe different trade-offs.
- **Decoupled feedback.** Each 3-minute trial yields one metabolic estimate and up to three 1-5 comfort ratings, since pilot subjects often changed their minds within a trial.
- **Controller.** Hip torque tracks a biological joint moment that a neural network estimates from IMUs and insoles. MO-HILBO tunes its delay (0-0.375 s) and the scale factors for positive and negative moment phases (0-0.3 each); the knee motors give zero torque.

## Results
- Simulation (50 runs per setting): MOBO reaches a lower IGD+ than NSGA-II within 40 queries at every noise level tested, and stays ahead for 1-4 action dimensions and 2-5 objectives.
- Human study: 24 trials per subject walking at 1.2 m/s, 3-4 hours per session. In 5-minute validation trials, three Pareto controllers dominated three anti-Pareto ones for every subject (hypervolume 4.02 vs 1.300, 1.55 vs 0.366, 1.02 vs 0.128).
- The surrogate predicted 17 of 18 (94%) pairwise orderings of held-out Pareto controllers; the one miss was a metabolic-cost ordering.
- All subjects found high delay neither comfortable nor efficient. The two first-time users found low torque most comfortable; the experienced user found high flexion and extension torque both comfortable and efficient.

## Limitations
Three participants, one session each, and only three hip parameters, with the knee left unpowered to keep sessions short. In simulation, four action dimensions were already harder for MOBO. The method assumes a person's objectives stay fixed, which may not capture adaptation, fatigue or shifting preferences.
