abstract: General-purpose mobile manipulation requires a combination of capable robot hardware, scalable demonstration collection, expressive whole-body control, and effective policy learning. Yet these components are often developed independently, creating mismatches that limit the capabilities of the complete system. We present Pixel, a co-designed full-stack system for learning general-purpose mobile manipulation. Pixel combines a low-cost wheeled humanoid with human-aligned kinematics and compliant grippers, multiple teleoperation and robot-free human data-collection interfaces, a unified whole-body controller, and a common policy learning pipeline across human and robot demonstrations. A hierarchical learning stack further enables language-conditioned visuomotor control and long-horizon task execution. We evaluate the system across hardware, data collection, and policy learning, and demonstrate mobile-manipulation behaviors requiring dexterity, active perception, navigation, and whole-body coordination. We hope that Pixel provides an integrated and extensible foundation for studying how robot hardware, data, control, and learning can be designed together for robot learning. ## What they do
Pixel is a mobile-manipulation stack built around one low-cost wheeled humanoid. The robot, its data-collection interfaces, the whole-body controller and a hierarchical policy were designed together. As a result, teleoperated demonstrations and robot-free human demonstrations map to the same actions on the same robot. The hardware costs under $15,000 and is open source.

## How it works
- **Robot.** A 23-DoF platform: holonomic swerve base, 430 mm vertical lift, torso pitch joint, two 7-DoF arms with human-like kinematics and a 2-DoF head. The single-DoF pincer grippers have compliant TPU and silicone fingers. Cameras sit on the head and both wrists.
- **Data collection.** Three teleoperation interfaces: a 65%-scale leader arm, VR with a joystick for the base, and VR with body tracking for the base. There is also a wearable rig under $1,000, with 386 g grippers that copy the robot's fingers and wrist cameras and a VR headset that tracks the head. All of them produce head and gripper pose targets for one shared whole-body controller based on geometric fabrics.
- **Policy.** A language-conditioned flow-matching transformer reads all three cameras. It predicts head and gripper poses in a "relative-base" frame placed on the ground under the head, which works for both human and robot data. A Gemini-based agent splits spoken requests into skills from a fixed library and monitors their progress.

## Results
- Average task progress is 82.0% from human demonstrations (toy cleanup, tidy pillow, trash disposal) and 78.7% from teleoperated ones (vacuuming, hanging a jacket, fetching a snack).
- Relative-base actions reach 82.0%, vs. 67.3% for UMI-style relative actions and 25.7% for a head-anchored frame. With all joints active the system scores 100% on the three human-data tasks; disabling the torso, lift or head drops it to 47.4%, 71.2% and 52.0%.
- In a 12-person study, every participant ranked the wearable rig first, and it was 4.2-6.2x faster than teleoperation. Adding teleoperated data to human data raised progress from 85.0% to 94.3%.

## Limitations
Pixel cannot climb stairs and has no dexterous hands. The wearable rig records only head and gripper poses: no other body contact (such as bracing a door with an elbow) and no touch. The high-level agent was not systematically evaluated and cannot go beyond its skill library. On teleoperated tasks, joint-space actions scored higher (86.3%) than the chosen relative-base actions.
