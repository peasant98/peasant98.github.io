abstract: 'Video world models for robots are increasingly conditioned on a simulated or rendered proxy of the scene, on the assumption that the video will show what the proxy shows. Testing this assumption requires a proxy whose state is explicit and can be perturbed. We present SimPWM (Simulator-as-Proxy World Model): given a calibrated camera, it represents, from the first frame alone, the robot by its 3D model and the objects the gripper may touch as 3D points; a rule-based simulator with no trained parameters moves them under the actions, and a video model renders the result. SimPWM returns the realistic video together with the simulated state. We then freeze the simulated objects while the arm moves as recorded and check whether the video still shows the object lifted. On DROID episodes from unseen setups, the answer differs for the arm and for the objects. With each of three base video models, the arm follows the proxy (robot IoU 0.75-0.78, against at most 0.69 for end-to-end models); rendering the robot alone already gives this. What the simulated objects add is the object state: against a robot-only proxy, they cut missed pick-ups from 54% to 38%, and the video follows them only with some base models: on rigid objects, freezing them lowers the lift rate of MiniMax-H3, fine-tuned on episodes whose simulation agrees with the real video, from 42% to 10%, but that of LTX-2.3, trained on the same episodes, only from 57% to 49%, as if it inferred the grasp from the arm motion; more than half of the remaining lifts of MiniMax-H3 are of cloth and rope, which the simulator carries rigidly. With any base model, the simulated state records what the simulation did; under perturbed actions, a video that disagrees with it reveals that the video ignores the simulated state.'

## What they do
End-to-end video world models often let the arm drift from the commands or make grasped objects split or stay behind, and nothing records what the actions did. SimPWM splits the job: a simple simulator computes what moves where, and a video model supplies the appearance. Each rollout returns a video plus an explicit simulated state, which the authors use to test whether the video follows it.

## How it works
- **Real-to-sim from one frame.** Given a calibrated camera, stereo depth (FoundationStereo, filled in with Depth Anything V2), a RANSAC support plane and SAM 2/SAM 3 segments turn the first frame into the robot mesh, object point sets and a background. No CAD models or object categories are needed.
- **Action snap.** Where the gripper closes on an object, the object's points are rescaled about the camera so that it sits at the gripper's depth.
- **Rule-based simulator.** Grasp the object between the jaws, carry it rigidly, drop it onto a height map on release. It has no trained parameters.
- **Sim-to-real.** The proxy (first frame with the robot and moved objects redrawn, unseen pixels in magenta) is the reference video for a fine-tuned MiniMax-H3, LTX-2.3 or Cosmos-Transfer 2.5.

## Results
- Tested on 293 DROID episodes from 121 setups unseen in training. Robot IoU is 0.75-0.78 for every base model, against at most 0.69 for end-to-end models. With the same base and data, the proxy nearly doubles Cosmos-Predict 2.5's robot IoU.
- The simulated grasp matches the real one in 69.5% of decidable episodes.
- Compared with a robot-only proxy, simulated objects cut missed pick-ups from 54.1% to 37.6%.
- With the simulated objects frozen, MiniMax-H3 lifts rigid objects in 10% of videos (42% under recorded actions), but LTX-2.3 still does in 49% (57%), as if inferring grasps from the arm motion.

## Limitations
Pushing, pouring, folding and articulation are not simulated, only objects visible in the first frame exist, and cloth and rope are carried rigidly. A closing point a few pixels off grasps a neighbouring object. The camera is calibrated per episode partly on test frames, which favours robot IoU. Actions are replayed open-loop, and the metrics rely on SAM tracks and a VLM judge. Generating one video takes about 5-26 minutes on an A100.
