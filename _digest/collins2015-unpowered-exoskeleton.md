abstract: 'With efficiencies derived from evolution, growth and learning, humans are very well-tuned for locomotion. Metabolic energy used during walking can be partially replaced by power input from an exoskeleton, but is it possible to reduce metabolic rate without providing an additional energy source? This would require an improvement in the efficiency of the human-machine system as a whole, and would be remarkable given the apparent optimality of human gait. Here we show that the metabolic rate of human walking can be reduced by an unpowered ankle exoskeleton. We built a lightweight elastic device that acts in parallel with the user''s calf muscles, off-loading muscle force and thereby reducing the metabolic energy consumed in contractions. The device uses a mechanical clutch to hold a spring as it is stretched and relaxed by ankle movements when the foot is on the ground, helping to fulfill one function of the calf muscles and Achilles tendon. Unlike muscles, however, the clutch sustains force passively. The exoskeleton consumes no chemical or electrical energy and delivers no net positive mechanical work, yet reduces the metabolic cost of walking by 7.2 ± 2.6% for healthy human users under natural conditions, comparable to savings with powered devices. Improving upon walking economy in this way is analogous to altering the structure of the body such that it is more energy-effective at walking. While strong natural pressures have already shaped human locomotion, improvements in efficiency are still possible. Much remains to be learned about this seemingly simple behavior.'

## What they do
Earlier devices lowered the energy cost of walking by adding power from pumps or motors. This paper asks whether a device with no energy source can. The authors built an ankle exoskeleton whose spring, held by a mechanical clutch while the foot is on the ground, works in parallel with the calf muscles and Achilles tendon and carries part of their force.

## How it works
- **Rationale.** Muscles spend metabolic energy to hold force even when they do no work; a clutch holds force for free. The design copies calf muscle fibers, which appear to act as a clutch holding the spring-like Achilles tendon.
- **Clutch.** A 0.057 kg ratchet and pawl with no motor, battery or computer, switched by ankle motion. It engages at heel strike, holds the spring as it stretches and recoils through stance, and releases it for toe clearance in swing.
- **Frame.** Carbon-fiber shank and foot sections molded for each participant and hinged at the ankle, 0.408-0.503 kg per leg.
- **Protocol.** Nine healthy adults walked on a treadmill at 1.25 m/s without the device, with it but no spring, and with five springs (130-400 N·m/rad), after 21 minutes of training per condition.

## Results
- Metabolic rate first fell, then rose with stiffness. The 180 N·m/rad spring cut net metabolic rate from 2.88 ± 0.10 to 2.67 ± 0.14 W/kg, a 7.2 ± 2.6% reduction (p = 0.023), similar to earlier powered devices.
- Wearing the device without a spring did not raise energy cost (p = 0.9), and joint angles changed little.
- With the 180 spring, average biological ankle moment fell 14% and mid-stance soleus activity 22%, mainly in the first 40% of the stride.
- Stiffer springs brought metabolic rate back to normal, apparently with more tibialis anterior activity in early and mid-stance and more plantarflexor activity at the end of stance.

## Limitations
Nine young, healthy participants walked at one speed on a treadmill, without blinding. Compliance in the frame, rope and human-device interface likely cut effective stiffness by about 33%, varying between subjects, and the clutch sometimes failed during training. Why stiff springs raise the cost is only partly explained; simple walking models predict center-of-mass changes that were not observed.
