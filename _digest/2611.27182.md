abstract: 'Agent harnesses are turn-based: the agent acts, waits for the result, then reasons. When the action invokes a pretrained robot policy, such as a vision-language-action (VLA) or world action model (WAM), the policy may react continuously to observations, but the agent cannot revisit its decision until the call returns. Meanwhile, a person may step into the robot''s path or move the object it is about to grasp. Invoking the agent more often narrows this blind interval, but a reasoning model takes seconds per decision and most control steps need no new one. We propose TRIP, a training-free, event-driven harness in which the agent programs its own interrupts. With each call, the agent writes a tripwire: a lightweight, stateful Python program that runs alongside the policy, can query a compact VLM (for example, to locate a person and measure their distance), and decides whether the call continues or control returns to the agent. When it fires, the harness halts the policy and returns control with the reason; the agent then reassesses, adapts, and continues under a rewritten tripwire. We evaluate TRIP with three VLAs and one WAM on RoboHD, a benchmark of 168 RoboCasa365 scenes in which a simulated person disturbs the robot and any human-robot contact counts as failure. Compared with a turn-based harness using the same agent and tools, TRIP halves the rate of human contact with the strongest policy, raising safety from 61.3% to 81.3%, without sacrificing task success (38.7% vs. 31.0%). Real-robot experiments show that TRIP completes two manipulation tasks under human interference where the turn-based harness and the policy alone largely fail.'

## What they do
In a turn-based agent harness, a reasoning model hands control to a pretrained policy (a VLA or world action model) and does not see the scene again until the call returns, even if a person has stepped into the robot's path or moved the target in the meantime. TRIP lets the agent write its own interrupt for each call. This "tripwire" is a small stateful Python program that watches the scene while the policy runs. The policy is used as is, without fine-tuning.

## How it works
- **Tripwires.** A tripwire runs at every control step in a sandbox. It asks a compact VLM (Qwen3.5-9B) questions such as where the person is, then combines the answers with depth, timers and memory. It either lets the call continue or returns a reason such as "person 0.6 m away, closing at 0.3 m/s".
- **Halt first, then reason.** When a tripwire fires, the harness stops the robot before waking the agent. The agent then resumes the policy, moves the robot directly or waits, and rewrites the tripwire to match, for example to watch for the person to leave.
- **RoboHD.** This new benchmark has 168 RoboCasa365 scenes in which a human avatar blocks the route, moves objects, changes fixtures or works nearby. Any contact with the person fails the episode.

## Results
- With the strongest policy (Xiaomi-Robotics-1), safety rises from 61.3% to 81.3% compared with a turn-based harness that uses the same agent, tools and prompt. Success with the person rises from 31.0% to 38.7%.
- Keeping the agent's first tripwire for the whole episode drops safety to 61.3%.
- On a real xArm 7, π0.5 policies hung a mug or a towel while a person interfered. TRIP succeeded 25 of 40 times, against 5 for the turn-based harness and 1 for the policy alone. A tripwire check takes 0.24 s and an agent turn 4.3 s.

## Limitations
In simulation the person is a scripted avatar with no collision body, and physics pauses while the agent and the VLM compute. One episode takes about 12 min of wall-clock time for 1.5 min of robot time. In the real-robot trials, one of the authors played the person, on two tasks.
