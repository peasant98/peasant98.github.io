abstract: 'LLM agents have made remarkable progress in robot manipulation, yet they suffer from expensive inference, dense step-by-step control, and tool bottlenecks. We introduce SOLVE, a training-free, versatile and efficient agent policy: a multimodal LLM agent writes a sparse, ordered sequence of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call, observes the result, and writes the next, while a motion planner interpolates between them. The agent decides the number and spacing of keyposes for both fast long-range motion and fine-grained manipulation. This granularity matches the sparsity of teleoperated manipulation: most demonstrations in DROID can be compressed into a few keyposes instead of dense actions. SOLVE grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries answered by noisy sources, such as multi-view triangulation, stereo, or monocular depth, and copes with that noise itself. It also improves from execution feedback, reflecting on its own rollouts and distilling a few rules per task. SOLVE reaches state-of-the-art performance of 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, with only $1.7 per episode on average, 12x less than previous dense-control agent policies. On a real robot, the same agent achieves an 88% success rate (75 of 85 episodes) over 16 challenging zero-shot tasks. Our project page is at https://serenos.github.io/solve-website.'

## What they do
SOLVE uses a frozen multimodal LLM (GPT-6 Astra or GPT-6.1 Sol) directly as a manipulation policy, with no training. Instead of one small step per call, or a program over a fixed primitive library, each call writes a short, ordered sequence of absolute 6-DoF end-effector keyposes. A motion planner moves the arm along straight lines between them, and the agent looks at the result before writing the next sequence.

## How it works
- **Why keyposes.** Compressing 57,534 DROID episodes, a grasp or release needs about 6 keyposes instead of 96 recorded actions (2 cm / 10° tolerance), and over 91% of travel lies on straight segments longer than 5 cm.
- **Keypose interface.** Up to five absolute keyposes per call (30 for drawing). The gripper acts on arrival, and the agent sets how fast each keypose is approached.
- **Sparse metric queries.** The agent names the few pixels its next keypose depends on. A tool returns their 3D points from epipolar matching across cameras, Depth Anything 3 scaled by the table plane, or stereo depth on hardware, each tagged with its source.
- **Experience notes.** On layouts separate from evaluation the agent reflects on its rollouts; notes from three runs are merged into at most five bounded rules per task.

## Results
- RoboDojo-Sim (two ARX-X5 arms, 42 tasks): 74.5% success in the official evaluation, 18.1 points above the best leaderboard entry; the same model driven step by step gets 22.5%.
- RoboLab (Franka, 120 tasks, no demonstrations): 84.5%, 27.4 points above the best entry.
- $1.7 per episode with GPT-6 Astra, 12x less than a step-wise agent. On 50 shared RoboDojo episodes: 74% vs. 26% success, 36 vs. 154 model calls.
- Real UR12e, zero-shot: 75 of 85 episodes (88%) over 16 tasks, including precision tasks (7/10), shirt folding (6/10) and Gomoku against a person (10/10).

## Limitations
Keyposes cannot supply force or continuous feedback, and most real-robot failures are contact problems: a shirt sliding with the gripper, or an insertion released before the pegs are seated. A model call takes a median 11.7 s against 6.1 s to execute its keyposes, which confines SOLVE to quasi-static scenes. Notes that keep evolving peak and then degrade, so they have to be merged and frozen.
