tldr: A Blender-based simulator turns 3D scenes and camera motion into the photon detections of a passive SPAD camera (shot noise, dark counts, crosstalk), with matching labels. abstract: Passive single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) cameras provide short-exposure observations for low-light perception, but collecting task annotations for these sensors remains difficult, and annotated datasets are scarce. We present a 3D simulation pipeline that generates realistic photon detections together with camera-motion and semantic labels, enabling perception models to be trained on synthetic SPAD observations and applied directly to real-world data. The pipeline couples controllable scene geometry and motion with a sensor-informed observation model that accounts for photon shot noise, dark counts, and spatial crosstalk. We evaluate the pipeline through calibrated photon-count comparisons and zero-shot sim-to-real transfer across three downstream perception tasks. We show that classifiers trained only on synthetic SPAD data achieve high accuracy on real low-light recordings at illumination levels less than 2 lux, without real-data fine-tuning. Similarly, we apply models trained entirely on simulated camera sweeps to real-world camera-rotation estimation and achieve mean absolute rotation errors of less than 1 degree on real 20-degree and 30-degree sweeps at 0.5 and 5.0 lux, when deployed zero-shot. We additionally train semantic segmentation models on synthetic data with automatically generated dense labels and perform direct inference on real-world low-light recordings; the results further demonstrate the usefulness of synthetic dense supervision for real low-light perception. Our simulator thus provides a practical source of annotated training data for real SPAD perception.

## What they do
Passive SPAD cameras count single photons in very short windows, so they can see moving scenes in light too dim for an RGB camera. Labeled SPAD data is scarce, and labels for camera motion or per-pixel segmentation are hard to collect on real hardware. The authors build a 3D simulator that renders a scene, converts it into photon detections, and exports aligned labels from the same scene state. Every model is trained and selected on synthetic data only, then tested on recordings from a real camera.

## How it works
- **Scenes and labels.** Objects, lighting and camera trajectories are set up in Blender and rendered with Cycles at sensor resolution, once per 1 ms interval. The scene state gives class labels, the camera rotation between the ends of a clip, and per-pixel object classes.
- **Photon model.** Rendered RGB becomes a detection rate through the sensor's measured detection-probability curve, a fill factor of 0.10 and a Gaussian blur. Counts are Poisson samples; dark counts come from a per-pixel rate field fitted to the sensor's dark measurements; each detection adds a neighbour event with probability 0.02 (crosstalk).
- **Inputs.** Detections are summed into normalized 1 ms count frames, with one model per light level. The real camera is a NovoViz NV04ASC-HW with a 64x48 SPAD array.

## Results
- Real and simulated count histograms of a static fan differ by a total variation distance of 0.134 at 1.85 lux and 0.020-0.025 at 0.40 to 0.05 lux.
- On 64 printed MNIST digits, real accuracy is 96.88%, 96.88% and 93.75% at 1.85, 0.40 and 0.15 lux, beating adapted Suonsivu et al. and VisionSIM sensor models by up to 9.38 and 54.69 points. Without dark counts, accuracy at 0.15 lux falls to 32.81%.
- Camera-rotation MAE on real 20 and 30 degree sweeps is 0.773-0.996 degrees at 0.5 and 5.0 lux.
- Segmentation of the four objects reaches 88.48% (0.5 lux) and 93.09% (5.0 lux) real mIoU, 2.51 and 1.13 points below synthetic test data.

## Limitations
The paper has no limitations section. Test scenes are simple (printed digits, four black primitives) and the sensor is 64x48 pixels. The simulated light intensity is tuned for each illumination level. Occluding object layouts, absent from training, drop segmentation to about 54% mIoU, with the same class confusions in real and simulated data. Rotation references are nominal sweep angles with up to 1 degree of placement uncertainty.
