abstract: Robots operating in complex environments occasionally encounter situations where autonomous navigation fails. Requesting and receiving a timely intervention from their human supervisor can be critical for failure prevention or recovery. However, requesting help too frequently or at inappropriate times can increase operator workload and reduce team efficiency, while delayed requests may lead to prolonged downtime or unrecoverable failures. In addition to alert timing, the human, robot and task context also influences the intervention outcomes. This paper proposes an algorithm for learning to generate adaptive human intervention requests during human-robot teaming. A help-seeking policy is trained via offline reinforcement learning based on past intervention helpfulness and autonomous progress. This learned alert policy enables a robot to selectively request human help when the intervention outcome is likely to be beneficial. We evaluate the proposed policy in different task environments and user expertise levels in simulation, followed by evaluation in a physical user study. Our findings demonstrate that the learned policy improves exploration efficiency, reduces unnecessary alerts, and enhances novice user support without compromising expert performance in real-time human-robot teaming missions.

## What they do
A robot exploring a tunnel-like or cluttered site sometimes gets stuck, and a remote operator can help by placing a waypoint. Fixed rules (e.g. "alert if the robot barely moves for 12 s") fire too often or too late. This paper learns *when* to ask: a binary alert policy trained with offline RL that requests help only when the robot is unlikely to recover alone and an intervention is likely to pay off.

## How it works
- **Inputs.** A sliding window of scalar features (velocity, pose change, explored area, time since last alert) goes through an MLP and GRU; a stack of local costmaps goes through a CNN.
- **Three heads.** A Q-value head picks alert or continue; two auxiliary heads predict autonomous progress and intervention helpfulness, both supervised from logged outcomes.
- **Training.** Conservative Q-learning on 134 logged semi-autonomous exploration episodes from a prior study, augmented with a learned novice/expert waypoint model (over 99,000 sequences). The reward favours exploration progress and helpful interventions and penalises unnecessary alerts.

## Results
- Simulation, 100 rollouts on unseen maps: with expert-derived interventions the policy explored 232.0 m² vs 199.0 m² for the threshold baseline, with recovery time 29.1 s vs 70.5 s. It also beat score-based and LinUCB baselines (p<0.001 on explored area).
- Physical study, 20 participants supervising a Spot robot outdoors for three 10-minute sessions: explored area 678 m² (learned) vs 596 m² (threshold) vs 569 m² (no alerts); alert success rate 0.63 vs 0.47.
- NASA-TLX workload was lowest with learned alerts (39.5 vs 43.8 threshold, 51.5 no alerts). Trust and perceived alert usefulness did not differ significantly.

## Limitations
Simulated users respond to alerts immediately, unlike real people. The physical study had a small, mostly novice sample and covered one exploration task and environment. The logs only support mission-level comparisons, not per-failure-type analysis.
