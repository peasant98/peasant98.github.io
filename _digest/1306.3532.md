abstract: 'In this paper we present a novel probabilistic sampling-based motion planning algorithm called the Fast Marching Tree algorithm (FMT*). The algorithm is specifically aimed at solving complex motion planning problems in high-dimensional configuration spaces. This algorithm is proven to be asymptotically optimal and is shown to converge to an optimal solution faster than its state-of-the-art counterparts, chiefly PRM* and RRT*. The FMT* algorithm performs a "lazy" dynamic programming recursion on a predetermined number of probabilistically-drawn samples to grow a tree of paths, which moves steadily outward in cost-to-arrive space. As a departure from previous analysis approaches that are based on the notion of almost sure convergence, the FMT* algorithm is analyzed under the notion of convergence in probability: the extra mathematical flexibility of this approach allows for convergence rate bounds--the first in the field of optimal sampling-based motion planning. Specifically, for a certain selection of tuning parameters and configuration spaces, we obtain a convergence rate bound of order $O(n^{-1/d+ρ})$, where $n$ is the number of sampled points, $d$ is the dimension of the configuration space, and $ρ$ is an arbitrarily small constant. We go on to demonstrate asymptotic optimality for a number of variations on FMT*, namely when the configuration space is sampled non-uniformly, when the cost is not arc length, and when connections are made based on the number of nearest neighbors instead of a fixed connection radius. Numerical experiments over a range of dimensions and obstacle configurations confirm our theoretical and heuristic arguments by showing that FMT*, for a given execution time, returns substantially better solutions than either PRM* or RRT*, especially in high-dimensional configuration spaces and in scenarios where collision-checking is expensive.'

## What they do
FMT* is an asymptotically optimal sampling-based motion planner for high-dimensional configuration spaces. It runs a "lazy" dynamic programming recursion over a fixed set of samples, growing a tree of paths steadily outward in cost-to-arrive, like the Fast Marching Method. The aim is far fewer collision checks than PRM* and RRT*.

## How it works
- **Lazy one-step connections.** Pop the lowest-cost open node from a heap. Each unvisited neighbor picks the open node that gives it the lowest cost-to-arrive, ignoring obstacles, and only that edge is collision-checked. If it collides, the sample waits for a later iteration.
- **Rare suboptimality.** This misses a better parent only near obstacles, and the fraction of such samples goes to zero as n grows.
- **Analysis in probability.** Proving optimality as convergence in probability yields a rate bound of order n^(-1/d+ρ) without obstacles. FMT* needs O(n log n) time but only O(n) collision checks in expectation, versus O(n log n) for PRM*.
- **Extensions** that stay optimal: non-uniform sampling, general costs and k-nearest neighbors.

## Results
- SE(2) bug trap: similar solution quality in about half the time of RRT* and a tenth that of PRM*. FMT* nears 100% success in about 1 s, RRT* in about 5 s, while PRM* is at 80% after 14 s.
- SE(3) maze: in under 5 s FMT* reaches a mean cost below what RRT* and PRM* reach in about 20 s and 70 s.
- 7D recursive maze: RRT* never found a solution in the allotted time; FMT* reaches low cost in about half the time of PRM*.
- With ADAPTIVE-SHORTCUT smoothing, FMT* gets within 10% of optimal with fewer than 1000 samples in a 5D maze.

## Limitations
FMT* runs on a fixed sample count, so it is not anytime. In the Alpha puzzle's narrow corridors it stops expanding when no sample lands inside, and reaches full success more slowly than RRT*. The neighbor scale and sample count need tuning per problem. The rate bound assumes no obstacles, and all experiments are simulated without differential constraints.
