tldr: Represent a visuomotor policy as a denoising diffusion process that generates a short sequence of future actions, executed with receding-horizon control. It beats prior behavior-cloning methods by 46.9% on average across simulation benchmarks and reaches 95% success on a real Push-T task.
abstract: This paper introduces Diffusion Policy, a new way of generating robot behavior by representing a robot's visuomotor policy as a conditional denoising diffusion process. We benchmark Diffusion Policy across 12 different tasks from 4 different robot manipulation benchmarks and find that it consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art robot learning methods with an average improvement of 46.9%. Diffusion Policy learns the gradient of the action-distribution score function and iteratively optimizes with respect to this gradient field during inference via a series of stochastic Langevin dynamics steps. We find that the diffusion formulation yields powerful advantages when used for robot policies, including gracefully handling multimodal action distributions, being suitable for high-dimensional action spaces, and exhibiting impressive training stability. To fully unlock the potential of diffusion models for visuomotor policy learning on physical robots, this paper presents a set of key technical contributions including the incorporation of receding horizon control, visual conditioning, and the time-series diffusion transformer. We hope this work will help motivate a new generation of policy learning techniques that are able to leverage the powerful generative modeling capabilities of diffusion models. Code, data, and training details is publicly available diffusion-policy.cs.columbia.edu

## What they do
Demonstration data is multimodal, temporally correlated and needs precision, which strains standard behavior cloning. Diffusion Policy models the policy as a conditional denoising diffusion process over robot actions: starting from Gaussian noise, a noise-prediction network refines a sequence of future actions over K steps, conditioned on recent observations. Because it learns the score gradient instead of an energy, it avoids the negative sampling that makes implicit policies such as IBC unstable to train.

## How it works
- **Receding-horizon action sequences.** From the last To observation steps the policy predicts Tp actions and executes Ta of them before replanning; 8 executed steps worked best for most tasks.
- **Observations as conditioning.** Images are encoded once by a ResNet-18 trained end-to-end, and only actions are denoised. With DDIM (10 inference steps) latency is 0.1 s on an Nvidia 3080.
- **Two denoisers.** A 1D temporal CNN with FiLM conditioning works out of the box on most tasks; a time-series transformer does better when actions change quickly but needs more tuning.
- **Position control.** Predicting positions consistently beat velocity commands for Diffusion Policy, while the baselines worked best with velocity.

## Results
- Simulation (Robomimic, Push-T, Block Pushing, Franka Kitchen): beats LSTM-GMM, IBC and BET on every task, by 46.9% on average; +213% on Kitchen's p4 metric and +32% on Block Push's p2.
- Real Push-T on a UR5: 95% success and 0.80 IoU (human demos 0.84), against 20% for LSTM-GMM and 0% for IBC.
- Mug flipping succeeds 90% of the time (LSTM-GMM failed to grasp in every trial). Sauce coverage is 0.74 for pouring and 0.77 for spreading, against 0.79 for humans.
- Bimanual Franka tasks, 20 trials each: egg beater 55%, mat unrolling 75%, shirt folding 75%.

## Limitations
It inherits behavior cloning's dependence on adequate demonstrations. Inference costs more and is slower than simpler policies such as LSTM-GMM; predicting action sequences partly offsets this, but may not suffice for tasks that need high-rate control.
