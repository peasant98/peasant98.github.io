## What they do
VR teleoperation gives indirect control without force feedback, and Mobile ALOHA's leader arms keep the operator away from the grippers while the powered base gets in the way. HALO has the operator move the robot arms by hand (kinesthetic teaching) while walking a light, base-free platform, then processes the recordings to match autonomous deployment.

## How it works
- **Manipulation.** A redesigned end-effector, less than half the original size with about 40° more pitch range, gives direct visual and force feedback.
- **Mobility.** During collection the differential-drive base is replaced by passive wheels plus two fixed-direction wheels on the drive axis, keeping the same nonholonomic constraint. Pushing it takes 2.3 N, vs 13.0 N for Mobile ALOHA.
- **Deployment alignment.** An affine map and an estimated response delay turn encoder-measured platform motion into base velocity commands; operator hands are segmented (SAM2) and inpainted out of the video.
- **Policy.** pi0.5 is fine-tuned per task to output arm joints and base velocity together, run with asynchronous real-time action chunking.

## Results
- With one operator, the same tasks and a one-hour budget, demonstrations are 2.9x (Push Chair) and 2.8x (Disassemble Lego) faster than with Mobile ALOHA, and Lego demonstration success is 20 points higher.
- Policies succeed 30/40 on Disassemble Lego and 11/15 on the six-stage box task. Policies trained on VR or Mobile ALOHA data from the same time budget fail both tasks.
- Inpainting the hands out raises Push Chair success from 55% to 85%.

## Limitations
The paper has no limitations section. The collection comparison uses a single operator, and policies are trained per task. Each dataset needs a hand-inpainting pass and a calibration of the passive platform against the powered base. Active perception (moving the camera viewpoint) is left to future work.
