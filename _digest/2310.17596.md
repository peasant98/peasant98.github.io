tldr: MimicGen turns about 10 human demos per task into 1000 new ones by re-posing object-centric pieces of them for new scenes and keeping only the replays that succeed. From ~200 human demos it generated 50K+ across 18 tasks; on Three Piece Assembly, agent success rises from 1.3% to 82.0%.
abstract: Imitation learning from a large set of human demonstrations has proved to be an effective paradigm for building capable robot agents. However, the demonstrations can be extremely costly and time-consuming to collect. We introduce MimicGen, a system for automatically synthesizing large-scale, rich datasets from only a small number of human demonstrations by adapting them to new contexts. We use MimicGen to generate over 50K demonstrations across 18 tasks with diverse scene configurations, object instances, and robot arms from just ~200 human demonstrations. We show that robot agents can be effectively trained on this generated dataset by imitation learning to achieve strong performance in long-horizon and high-precision tasks, such as multi-part assembly and coffee preparation, across broad initial state distributions. We further demonstrate that the effectiveness and utility of MimicGen data compare favorably to collecting additional human demonstrations, making it a powerful and economical approach towards scaling up robot learning. Datasets, simulation environments, videos, and more at https://mimicgen.github.io .

## What they do
Imitation learning needs many teleoperated demonstrations, yet much of that data repeats the same skill in a different place. MimicGen takes a handful of human demos (10 per task) and automatically generates large datasets for wider object placements, new object instances of the same category and different robot arms. BC-RNN policies are then trained on the generated data.

## How it works
- **Object-centric segments.** Each task is a known sequence of subtasks, each relative to one object (grasp mug, place it on the machine, ...). Source demos are split where each subtask ends.
- **Transform and stitch.** For a new scene, pick a source segment, read its delta-pose actions as end-effector target poses and transform them so the pose relative to the object's new pose stays the same. A linear interpolation segment bridges from the robot's current pose to the start of the segment.
- **Execute and filter.** The robot executes the new trajectory; only attempts that complete the task are kept.

## Results
- 50K+ demos for 18 tasks in robosuite and Isaac Gym Factory from ~200 human demos. Training on 1000 generated D0 demos instead of the 10 source demos: Square 11.3% to 90.7%, Threading 19.3% to 98.0%. Agents on the broader D1 distributions reach 42-99%.
- 200 MimicGen demos (from 10 human) give agents comparable to 200 human demos. Going from 200 to 1000 generated demos helps a lot; 5000 adds little.
- Panda demos generate data for Sawyer, IIWA and UR5e: generation rates differ (38-74% on Square D0) but policies reach 80-91% on all four arms. Factory assembly policies reach 82-99% on D0 vs 9-15% from the source demos.
- Real robot: generation succeeds 82.3% (Stack) and 52.1% (Coffee); trained agents score 36% and 14% over 50 trials on the wide distribution, vs 0% for agents trained on the 10 source demos.

## Limitations
Needs the subtask sequence, one reference object per subtask and object poses during generation (not at deployment). Interpolation is not collision-aware; the authors suggest the longer interpolation used on hardware (50 steps vs 5) may explain the lower real-world success. Filtering only on task success can leave biased data. Shown only on quasi-static, single-arm tasks with rigid objects from one category.
