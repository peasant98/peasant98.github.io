abstract: We present SurgeCount, a system for auditable robotic surgical sponge counting that composes learned skills under a written procedural protocol. Retained sponges cause preventable harm, and surgical teams count every sponge before closure to reduce that risk. The robot repeatedly isolates one deformable sponge from a tangled pile and places it in the next numbered counting-tray slot, requiring handling of occluded grasp threads while preserving step order. We train one subtask-conditioned policy on eleven short skills and learn when each skill ends. At each learned boundary, a stateful vision-language model selects the next skill from an operator-authored specification of step order, visual verification, and recovery. This creates temporal commitment to each subtask and enables failures to be recovered by recomposing existing skills rather than using a dedicated recovery policy. We evaluate five methods across 100 real-robot trials using 20 matched layouts. SurgeCount fills 47 of 60 ordered slots and completes 12 of 20 procedures, compared with 27 of 60 and 3 of 20 for the strongest of four end-to-end policies trained on the same data. Replacing the detailed protocol prompt with a minimal prompt reduces performance to 29 of 60 slots and 5 of 20 completed procedures. These results show that long-horizon performance depends not only on visuomotor capacity, but also on how learned skills are committed, verified, and composed. This tabletop proof of concept motivates further evaluation of protocol-guided robotic sponge counting in clinically representative settings.

## What they do
Surgical teams count every sponge before closing a wound. In SurgeCount, two SO-101 arms take three overlapping, red-stained sponges and move them one at a time into counting-tray slots I, II and III, in order. Each sponge has a blue radiopaque thread that serves as the grasp landmark. When the thread is hidden, the left arm first lifts a sponge edge to expose it. A single end-to-end policy does not run the whole task. Instead, a VLM chains short learned skills according to a written protocol.

## How it works
- **One policy, eleven skills.** A single π0 checkpoint is fine-tuned on 2,066 teleoperated episodes, each tagged with one of eleven single-arm subtasks. The tag's description serves as the language prompt. Offline DAgger adds operator corrections from the states where the policy itself fails.
- **Learned stop signal.** A scalar head trained on the last 10 frames of each subtask ends a skill after 10 consecutive stop predictions. The robot therefore commits to a skill instead of re-deciding at every action chunk.
- **Protocol-guided VLM.** At each stop, Gemini 3.1 Pro gets three camera frames, a compact state and the episode's chat history. It picks the next skill using an operator-written prompt that lists legal orderings, two strategies and a visual check for each skill.
- **Recovery by recomposition.** When a check fails, the VLM chains existing release-and-return skills and retries. There is no separate recovery policy.

## Results
- Each method ran 20 matched layouts. SurgeCount filled 47/60 slots and completed 12/20 procedures. The end-to-end baselines scored: π0 27/60 and 3/20, π0.5 15/60 and 2/20, SmolVLA 12/60 and 0/20, ACT 7/60 and 0/20.
- π0.5 changes its predicted subtask 18.2 times per trial between consecutive chunks.
- Grasps stay unreliable: 53.6% succeed on visible threads and 32.7% after a reveal. SurgeCount averages 4.3 retries per trial. The VLM misjudged 8 of 481 subtask outcomes (1.7%).
- With a minimal prompt, results drop to 29/60 and 5/20, and VLM misjudgments rise to 10.6%.

## Limitations
This is a tabletop proof of concept with a single workflow. Robustness to lighting, camera placement, tray geometry and sponge appearance is not tested. Missed thread grasps are retried, not prevented. The comparison cannot separate temporal commitment from the added VLM and protocol. The prompt ablation's difference in completed procedures is not significant (p≈0.07). Each VLM call takes 8.85 s on average.
