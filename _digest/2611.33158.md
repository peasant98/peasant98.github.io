abstract: 'A single policy that controls diverse robots must not only transfer task knowledge across embodiments, but also adapt its behavior to each robot''s capabilities. Existing approaches facilitate transfer through shared action representations, but these representations do not necessarily capture how differences in robot morphology should shape manipulation strategies. We introduce OmniAct, a cross-embodiment manipulation policy that conditions directly on each robot''s kinematics in a specific scene to produce embodiment-specific behavior. This kinematic graph represents the robot''s joint positions, motion axes, and connectivity to its visual observation, allowing the policy to account for how its embodiment related to objects and surfaces in the scene. A flow-matching policy predicts relative joint motions for each node, enabling control across robots with different degrees of freedom and kinematic configurations. ## What they do
Cross-embodiment policies usually share one action space so task knowledge transfers, but two arms may need different grasps or approaches to do the same task. OmniAct gives a single policy each robot's own kinematics as seen from the cameras, and trains it on simulated data from a very large set of procedurally generated arms so it learns how morphology should change the strategy.

## How it works
- **Scene-grounded kinematic graph.** Each joint, gripper tip and tool center point becomes a token holding its type, normalized joint position, and its center, motion axis and pose in each camera's 3D frame. Graph distances enter attention as biases, and view embeddings tie these tokens to the SigLIP 2 image tokens of the same camera.
- **Variable-DoF actions.** Nodes are padded into 12 fixed slots. A 400M-parameter flow-matching Transformer predicts 31-step chunks of relative joint motions and end-effector pose updates, and the first 8 steps are executed.
- **Embodiment scale.** Random seeds generate morphology, joint limits, dynamics and appearance. Self-collision and IK grasp checks reject most candidates. A local planner in MolmoSpaces then produces trajectories, and only successful ones are kept: 1.03M trajectories over 800K arms in six task families.

## Results
- On held-out arms and scenes in simulation (2,000 episodes, 500 steps), OmniAct reaches 63.0%, against 57.9% for UMI with all camera views and 17.8% for π0.5. On the task families both ran, it gets 64.7% against 41.1% for GPT-6 Astra.
- Zero-shot on real arms (20 pick-and-place setups each), it scores 14/20 on a Franka, the same as MolmoAct2-DROID. On an OpenYAM with an unseen fine-tip gripper it scores 13/20, against 7/20 for MolmoAct2-BimanualYAM and 0/20 for every other baseline.
- Ablations: training on 1/8 of the embodiments costs 26.8 points and dropping the graph tokens costs 5.1.

## Limitations
Only single arms are covered. The authors leave bimanual arms and humanoids open. The real-robot test is a single pick-and-place task. The graph adds only 5.1 points in simulation. Its larger effect far from the training data is shown on one arm, OpenYAM, where UMI with all views (same data, no graph) scores 0/20.
