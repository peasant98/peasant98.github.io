abstract: 'Experimental robot optimization often requires evaluating each candidate policy for seconds to minutes. The chosen evaluation time influences optimization because of a speed-accuracy tradeoff: shorter evaluations enable faster iteration, but are also more subject to noise. Here, we introduce a supplement to the CMA-ES optimization algorithm, named Adaptive Sampling CMA-ES (AS-CMA), which assigns sampling time to candidates based on predicted sorting difficulty, aiming to achieve consistent precision. We compared AS-CMA to CMA-ES and Bayesian optimization using a range of static sampling times in four simulated cost landscapes. AS-CMA converged on 98% of all runs without adjustment to its tunable parameter, and converged 24-65% faster and with 29-76% lower total cost than each landscape''s best CMA-ES static sampling time. As compared to Bayesian optimization, AS-CMA converged more efficiently and reliably in complex landscapes, while in simpler landscapes, AS-CMA was less efficient but equally reliable. We deployed AS-CMA in an exoskeleton optimization experiment and found the optimizer''s behavior was consistent with expectations. These results indicate that AS-CMA can improve optimization efficiency in the presence of noise while minimally affecting optimization setup complexity and tuning requirements.'

## What they do
Human-in-the-loop exoskeleton tuning measures each candidate controller for minutes, and the measurement time trades speed against noise. Usually every condition gets the same fixed time. AS-CMA leaves CMA-ES unchanged but picks a separate sample time for each candidate before it is evaluated, aiming for consistent ranking precision without ever re-measuring a candidate.

## How it works
- **Rank-based precision.** CMA-ES only uses the order of candidates, so each one needs just enough precision to be ranked against its nearest-cost neighbor. AS-CMA estimates that cost gap from the parameter-space distance to the nearest candidate, scaled by a local slope fit to the previous generation's pairwise cost differences.
- **Noise target to time.** It picks the noise level that gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 1.3, then inverts a measured noise-vs-time model to get the sample time (0.5-5.5 min, i.e. 34.2% to 0.4% metabolic error). Beyond that model it needs only rough cost bounds for the first generation.
- **Evaluation.** 100 runs per strategy on four simulated landscapes with multiplicative noise: a 4D ankle-exoskeleton cost model, 4D Rosenbrock, 4D Levy and 20D Sphere. Fine convergence means staying within 5% of the true minimum.

## Results
- Coarse convergence in 400/400 runs, fine in 393/400. Against each landscape's best fixed time (2-4 min), fine convergence came 24-65% sooner (not significant on Levy) at 29-76% lower cumulative cost.
- Bayesian optimization was faster on the simple Ankle and Sphere landscapes (71% and 50% less time) but reached fine convergence in only 90% and 42% of Rosenbrock and Levy runs. KL-KG resampling CMA-ES was 13% faster on 20D Sphere but took 294-564% more time elsewhere.
- One-participant ankle exoskeleton pilot: 42% lower metabolic cost than zero-torque walking (39% expected), with sample times spanning nearly the full range.

## Limitations
It needs a known link between measurement time and noise; if noise depends on other factors it does not help. Comparisons are simulated: the human pilot had one participant and no fixed-time control, and a power analysis puts a proper comparison at 71 participants.
