abstract: Humanoid box pushing is challenging when the physical properties of the task, such as the box mass and the ramp inclination, are unknown before the push begins. This paper presents PushMimic, a framework that learns adaptive humanoid box pushing from a single level-ground kinematic reference motion. The learned policy adapts its pushing posture to these properties without observing them. PushMimic augments the reference motion into a dynamically feasible motion dataset over these properties with sampling-based trajectory optimization, and distills the dataset into one motion tracking policy in one stage of training. Simulation results over box masses from 5 to 15 kg and ramp inclinations up to 15 degrees show that PushMimic raises the success rate from 57.8% to 88.8% compared with a baseline that tracks the original reference motion. It also reduces the cost of push of the arms by 22%. Hardware experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid show that the policy adapts its torso pitch during continuous pushing on an inclined treadmill, with box masses up to 19 kg and ramp inclinations up to 12.6 degrees. PushMimic therefore needs only one reference motion, instead of one for every box mass and ramp inclination.

## What they do
A humanoid pushing a heavy box up a ramp should lean further forward as the box gets heavier or the ramp steeper, but recorded human pushing motions are few and on level ground. PushMimic starts from a single level-ground reference motion and learns one Unitree G1 policy that adapts its posture to box mass and ramp inclination without observing either.

## How it works
- **Dynamic motion augmentation.** Sampling-based trajectory optimization (cross-entropy search over joint-target knots, scored on physics rollouts) re-solves the nominal motion for every point on a grid of 5-15 kg boxes and 0-15° ramps: 176 reference motions, about 9 min each on a desktop CPU.
- **Posture priors.** A torso pitch offset that grows with inclination, faster for heavier boxes (motivated by a planar quasi-static model), and a foot dead zone that lets the feet step back down the ramp.
- **Reference distillation.** The PPO policy observes only the nominal motion plus robot and box states, but is rewarded for tracking the augmented motion of its own task parameters, so it has to infer mass and slope from how the box responds. The critic sees the task parameters as privileged input.
- **Hardware.** The stock hands are replaced by flat plates on passive damped hinges; the box pose comes from motion capture.

## Results
- Simulation (MuJoCo, 3 seeds): 88.8% success vs 57.8% for a baseline that tracks only the nominal motion; the push force at 50% success rises from 66 N to 92 N, and the arms' cost of push drops 22%.
- Posture adaptiveness (1 = matches the augmented dataset) is 0.99 for inclination and 0.83 for mass, against 0.28 and -0.20 for the baseline.
- On a treadmill at 10°, adding weights (10 to 15 to 19 kg, beyond the 15 kg training range) raises torso pitch from 46.8° to 48.4° and 52.8°. Tilting from 0° to 12.6° raises it from 39.1° to 48.3°.
- Nine continuous hardware runs covered 8.5 min and 307 m of belt without a fall.

## Limitations
The reference grid grows exponentially with the number of task parameters, so adding box size or push distance would need sampled rather than gridded parameters. The box state comes from motion capture, which limits deployment to instrumented spaces.
