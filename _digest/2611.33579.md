abstract: A growing body of recent work addresses the retargeting of human hand-object demonstrations to robotic hands. To train whole-robot policies, however, the retargeted motions must be kinematically feasible and physically executable, and be produced efficiently at scale. We present DexEngine, a simulation pipeline that turns human manipulation data into train-ready, physically executable arm-hand trajectories. DexEngine first places each clip's hand-object motion where the arms can reach it, finds an arm motion that follows the human hands, and then corrects the motion in physics simulation with residual reinforcement learning. From nine human hand-object datasets, we generate about 5,200 clips for each of four dexterous hands, augmented into 65,000 trajectories (116 h) and 325,000 episodes (578 h) in total. Even with arms attached, DexEngine tracks objects more accurately than prior floating-hand methods and runs over 10x faster than RL-based ones. Policies trained on our data far outperform those trained on the same clips without physics correction or with the arms attached after a floating-hand correction, showing that fine-tuning data need physics correction. Mid-training on our data further improves them at equal fine-tuning budget, in simulation and on a real robot.

## What they do
Human hand-object datasets are easy to collect, but retargeted robot data usually ignores either physics (fingers pass through objects) or the arm (floating hands at wrist poses no arm can reach). DexEngine is a pipeline that turns these captures into physically executable trajectories for a whole bimanual robot: an RB-Y1 with 7-DoF arms on a fixed torso, fitted with any of four dexterous hands (SharpA, Wuji 2, XHand Pro, Tesollo). From nine human datasets it produces about 65,000 trajectories (116 h), rendered as 325,000 episodes with contact forces and head-camera depth.

## How it works
- **Arms before physics.** For each clip it searches placements of the demonstrated scene relative to the robot. A precomputed reachability map screens out most candidates, and full arm IK runs only on the survivors. A coarse path chosen by dynamic programming keeps the IK on a single solution branch, and the motion is then retimed to stay within joint velocity limits.
- **Residual RL correction.** One PPO policy per bundle of up to 64 clips outputs corrections to the wrist pose and finger joints, and differential IK moves the arm. At random-start resets, the arm goes back to the precomputed reference instead of being re-solved.
- **Recording and augmentation.** The best rollout of each accepted clip is recorded with actions, contact forces, stereo head-camera images with depth, and an exocentric view. Each clip is then rerun at new placements and rendered under five appearances.

## Results
- On 64 TACO clips, the arm-mounted version with one policy for all 64 clips reaches ADD-AUC 0.90 at 3.9 GPU-minutes per clip. On floating hands, CHORD gets 0.87 at 118 GPU-minutes and FlashDexRetarget 0.86 at 44.7.
- Placement search keeps a usable reference for 43.8% of 128 sampled clips, versus 1.6% at the default scene position.
- Simulated pick-and-place, pour and sweep: GR00T N1.7 averages 65% success at training placements and 50% at held-out ones, versus 6% and 5% on kinematic replays of the same clips. Mid-training on all the generated data raises this to 76% and 57%.
- Real robot, with no real training data: open-loop replay succeeds in 87% of trials (80% at held-out placements). GR00T with mid-training reaches 73% (27%), versus 20% (0%) without it.

## Limitations
RL-based physics correction is still costly. Only about a third of clips become robot data, mostly because the fixed-torso, fixed-base arms cannot follow the motion at any placement. The real-robot test covers one clip per task with five trials per placement level.
