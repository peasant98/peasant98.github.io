## What they do
A Shadow Dexterous Hand solves a Rubik's cube with control and vision models trained only in simulation. A standard solver plans the moves; the learned system senses the cube's pose and face angles and executes each flip and quarter-turn. The key ingredient is automatic domain randomization (ADR): a curriculum that starts from one calibrated environment and widens the randomization ranges whenever the model performs well enough.

## How it works
- **ADR.** Each randomized parameter (friction, cube size, latency, observation noise, rendering) has a uniform range. Workers fix one parameter at a range boundary and evaluate; above 20 successes per episode the bound moves outward, below 10 it moves back.
- **Policy.** PPO trains an LSTM policy with an asymmetric critic that sees privileged simulator state, for months on 64 V100 GPUs and 920 worker machines (about 13 thousand years of experience).
- **State estimation.** A three-camera ResNet50 model trained on ADR-rendered images predicts cube pose and face angles; a corner cut from each center sticker makes face angles readable from one frame. Most experiments read face angles from a sensorized Giiker cube instead.

## Results
- Real Rubik's cube (10 trials, fixed 43-subgoal scramble): the best ADR policy averages 26.8 successes, completing the full scramble in 20% of trials and half in 60%. Manual DR averages 1.8. With vision-only face angles the same policy averages 12.8 and never completes it.
- Real block reorientation: mean successes rise with ADR entropy from 1.4 to 32.0, almost 2x the earlier baseline (18.8). Manual DR retrained on the current setup scores 42.5 in simulation but 2.7 on hardware.
- ADR reaches high sim2sim performance faster than any fixed-randomization run.
- Signs of meta-learning: time per flip falls by about 1.6 s during a trial, and LSTM states predict cube size with over 80% accuracy.

## Limitations
Face angles from vision alone are much less reliable, so the headline result relies on a sensorized cube, and fingertips are tracked by motion capture. The policy sometimes gets stuck on face rotations and seems more likely to drop the cube early in a trial or after being stuck. Robustness to gloves, tied fingers and occlusion was only shown qualitatively.
