## What they do
Force-controlled arms that are safe around people cost tens of thousands of dollars. This paper argues that quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuation, low-ratio transmissions that stay backdrivable, can give a compliant, human-scale arm at the price of a research computer. The prototype, Blue, is a 7-DoF arm with a 2 kg payload, designed for teleoperation and learning rather than industrial precision.

## How it works
- **QDD actuation.** Brushless outrunner gimbal motors drive each joint through a single-stage 7.125:1 timing belt reduction. Motor current is measured for torque control, so the motor also acts as the torque sensor.
- **Differential links.** Each link combines two belt transmissions in a geared differential to produce pitch and roll. Belts let the motors sit closer to the shoulder, cutting gravity torque and flying inertia by about 30%.
- **Burst-power sizing.** Like a human arm, Blue is sized for peak loads at a limited duty cycle instead of a continuous rated payload.
- **Control.** A central computer sends torque commands over RS485 at 170 Hz; each motor driver runs current control at 20 kHz. A VR interface teleoperates the arm.

## Results
- Repeatability: over 133 return-to-home motions, all trials fell within a 3.7 mm radius, with 2.6 mm average deviation.
- Bandwidth: 7.5 Hz nominal position bandwidth and an estimated 13.8 Hz torque bandwidth, against a 2.3 Hz human limit.
- Thermal: 20 W RMS in normal motion without payload; a 2 kg payload can be held at full extension for about 2 minutes before a 7 minute rest.
- Cost: $3328 bill of materials per arm in a 7-unit run; contract-manufacturer quotes keep the end-user price within $5000 above 1500 arms.

## Limitations
Step responses are underdamped, with up to 2.8 cm of end-effector overshoot at a 2 kg payload. Friction and motor cogging give a torque hysteresis band of up to 2.6 Nm, which bounds the accuracy of commanded torque. Full payload is thermally limited to short bursts. The authors list timing belt skips and long-term creep of the plastic shells as open problems. Teleoperation tasks are evaluated only qualitatively.
