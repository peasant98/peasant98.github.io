## What they do
An image can be loaded and shown with a few lines of OpenCV, but 3D data has no equally easy library; the authors describe PCL as bloated and largely dormant. Open3D is written from a clean slate around two principles, usefulness and ease of use: a small set of dependencies, one community-standard solution per problem, and everything exposed in both C++ and Python. It is released under the MIT license.

## How it works
- **Data.** PointCloud, TriangleMesh and Image (plus RGBDImage), with numpy access to the underlying arrays and depth-image readers for NYU, TUM, SUN3D and Redwood.
- **Nine modules**, including odometry, registration (FPFH + RANSAC global registration, point-to-point and point-to-plane ICP, pose-graph multiway registration), volumetric integration and an OpenGL visualizer with animation and key callbacks.
- **Dependencies.** Eigen, GLFW, FLANN and pybind11. Boost and Ceres are left out in favor of in-house Gauss-Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt solvers, and dependency sources ship with the library so it compiles on Linux, OS X and Windows.
- **Speed.** Key steps were profiled and optimized, and many functions use OpenMP, including a parallel reduction for the Jacobian products in the least-squares solvers.

## Results
- A load, downsample and estimate-normals workflow is about half as long in Python as in Open3D C++, and about five times shorter than with PCL.
- ICP is up to 25x faster than PCL's. The reimplemented Choi et al. scene reconstruction pipeline is up to an order of magnitude faster than the original, and OpenMP speeds up the most expensive functions 3-6x on a modern CPU.
- A complete RGB-D scene reconstruction (fragments, global alignment, integration) is a Python script on top of the library, demonstrated on SceneNN. Open3D also runs in the Tanks and Temples evaluation server.

## Limitations
A short system paper. Speed-ups are given as "up to" factors, with no benchmark tables or hardware details, and are compared only with PCL and the original reconstruction code. The algorithm set is limited on purpose: only problems of broad interest are covered, and a new algorithm is added only if it is significantly stronger on a well-known benchmark.
