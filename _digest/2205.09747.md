## What they do
Testing human-to-robot handovers usually needs a person in the loop, and every study picks its own objects and metrics. HandoverSim is a simulation benchmark where a Franka Panda takes objects from a simulated human hand that follows real motion capture. The authors call it the first simulation benchmark for human-to-robot handovers.

## How it works
- **Giver.** Each of DexYCB's 1,000 captures (10 subjects, 20 YCB objects, 5 trials each) becomes a PyBullet scene. The MANO hand and the object follow their captured trajectories directly. The object has its own actuators and hand-object collisions are switched off. At the end of the capture, the hand holds still and waits.
- **Release.** The object is let go once both gripper fingers have touched it for 0.1 s. It also drops if it touches only other parts of the robot for 0.1 s. After that, only gravity and the robot act on it.
- **Task.** A Gym environment whose actions are 9-D joint targets for a PD controller. Success: the gripper holds the object inside a goal sphere for 0.1 s. Failure: the robot touches the hand, the object drops, or 13 s pass.
- **Splits.** Scenes with two objects the gripper cannot handle are dropped. The remaining 900 are split four ways: default, unseen subjects, unseen handedness and unseen objects.

## Results
- Baselines get ground-truth states and no training on the benchmark. On the default split, OMG Planner succeeds 62.50% of the time, but 27.78% of runs end in hand contact and it takes 9.72 s on average. Yang et al. succeeds 64.58% (17.36% contact, 4.90 s). GA-DDPG succeeds 50.00% when it waits for the hand to stop and 36.81% when it does not.
- In a real 6-person study, Yang et al. beat GA-DDPG (82% vs 80% success). It does so in simulation too (62.78% vs 55.00% on unseen subjects), and it is faster in both.
- Success is higher in the real study (GA-DDPG: 80% vs 55.00%). The authors attribute this to cooperative users adjusting the object.

## Limitations
The simulated human never reacts to the robot. Hand motion during and after the release is not simulated because no data exists for it. The grasp is not physically simulated: the object moves on its own actuators. Baselines see perfect states, so the reported times include no perception latency.
