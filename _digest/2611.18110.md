abstract: Reaching a goal state in a complex environment often requires a robot to overcome challenging terrain and obstacles. Which routes are accessible depends on the robot's physical design, the control policy it uses, and how it modifies its surroundings. We present a kinodynamic rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT) planner that uses physics simulation to exploit skills encoded in the control policy, which allows it to find a path in the scene with respect to the robot's capabilities. The planner samples states from a reduced state space in 3D around the scene and expands the RRT toward each sample using policy rollouts. We guide sampling with a preplanned path for a simple mass-point model to focus exploration on promising regions leading toward the goal state, reducing planning time by up to 63%. Simulation experiments with quadrupeds, humanoids, and wheeled robots demonstrate that our approach supports drop-in integration of various robots and control policies, exploiting their physical and policy capabilities without modifying the underlying planning algorithm. Predefined environmental modifications, such as placing a plank across a gap to build a bridge, will automatically be picked up by the planner as the robot's reachable space expands.

## What they do
Whether a robot can reach a goal depends on its body, its control policy and the current scene. PoliMop is a kinodynamic RRT planner that puts a physics simulator (MuJoCo) and the robot's deployed locomotion policy inside the search loop, so the tree only grows where that robot-policy pair can actually go. It samples in 3D rather than on a 2.5D elevation map, so it plans across overlapping levels. Objects moved by prescribed task-plan steps, such as a plank placed as a bridge, stay moved for the rest of the search.

## How it works
- **Reduced state, full snapshots.** Samples are compared by center-of-mass position, velocity and heading. Each node also stores the full simulator state so expansion can resume from it. A rollout is kept only if the robot stays upright and avoids disallowed collisions.
- **Steering.** A high-level controller (the Aicardi unicycle law by default) turns each sample into speed and yaw-rate commands. A pretrained policy (Go2 parkour, Unitree G1 hiking) or Guided Monte Carlo turns them into actuator inputs.
- **Path-guided sampling.** A mass-point model with a set jump height first plans a guide path. Half the samples come from its next unreached waypoints, and the rest stay global so a wrong guide can be abandoned. Dead-end leaves are pruned.

## Results
- Over 660 problems per configuration, guided sampling succeeds in 99.7% vs 95.6% unguided, with 27.5 s mean planning (+6.3 s preplanning) vs 92.4 s, about 63% less. A deliberately misleading guide still gets 92.6%, at 129.8 s.
- With tuned per-robot settings, all 830 problems across obstacle, elevation, maze and flat scenes were solved.
- On a high stair only the G1 hiking policy succeeded. On ramp and low stair the car reached 90% and 70%, while the plain go2 setup failed every trial.
- Without the prescribed plank and block placements no robot reached the goal within 30,000 iterations; with them every robot-scene pair succeeded in 5 of 5 runs.

## Limitations
Simulation only, never run on physical robots. The task plan is fixed and given, and pick and place are assumed to work. It needs robot-specific tuning, has no completeness guarantee, and assumes the mass-point guide transfers to the robot.
