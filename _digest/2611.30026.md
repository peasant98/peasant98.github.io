abstract: A compact yet agile and powerful limb is essential for amphibious legged robots that transition between spine-based swimming and leg-based terrestrial walking, as salamanders do. Salamander limbs combine large ranges of motion (ROM), multiple degrees of freedom (DoFs), and high load capacity. These features enable swinging and body-weight support during walking, as well as folding alongside the body during swimming. Their proximal kinematics can be represented by three rotational DoFs at the hip and one at the knee. Replicating these capabilities within a compact robotic limb is challenging with direct-drive architectures, because multiple joint-mounted actuators must be sufficiently spaced or placed distally to avoid self-collision, increasing limb size and rotational inertia. Here we present a cable-driven 4-DoF salamander robotic limb with a spatial cable-routing architecture that relocates actuators proximally while preserving large ROM and load capacity. Transmission-induced coupling is mitigated by mechanically compensating multi-segment cable paths, model-based feedforward decoupling, and output-side joint angle feedback. Compared with a direct-drive limb, the proposed design reduces mass by 20.4%, projected envelope area by 20.5%, and estimated effective yaw-axis inertia by 31.9%. Experiments verify independent, decoupled control over joint-angle ranges of 45 deg, 118 deg, 118 deg, and 135 deg for hip pitch, yaw, roll, and knee flexion/extension, respectively. During lifting tests, the limb supported an equivalent vertical load of at least 5.4 times its own weight. The proposed limb provides a compact, biologically representative platform for future studies of salamander locomotion and neural control.

## What they do
Salamander limbs swing and carry body weight on land and fold along the trunk for swimming, using three hip DoFs (yaw, pitch, roll about axes meeting at one point) and a knee. Most salamander robots simplify each limb to one or two DoFs. Earlier 3-DoF hips mounted a servo at every joint, which forced the motors apart and raised limb size and inertia. This paper builds a 230 mm, 794 g cable-driven limb that keeps the full 4-DoF chain and moves the hip motors toward the trunk.

## How it works
- **Cable transmissions.** Every joint is driven by an antagonistic pair of 0.8 mm Dyneema cables, with reduction ratios of 4.22, 1.50, 2.79 and 1.50 (hip pitch, yaw, roll, knee) so smaller Dynamixel XM430 servos suffice. Hip pitch adds a gear stage.
- **Built-in pretensioning.** Ratchet and screw-slider tensioners sit inside load-bearing parts, so no separate tensioning assemblies are needed.
- **Decoupling.** The roll cable passes through the pitch and yaw joints. Free-spinning idler and redirection pulleys keep its total length constant, and a geometric feedforward term cancels the remaining differential-length coupling.
- **Feedback.** Hall-effect sensors at each joint output close a 50 Hz loop, used in the yaw and roll load tests.

## Results
- Against a direct-drive limb: 20.4% less mass, 20.5% less projected envelope area, and 31.9% lower effective yaw inertia, identified from torque versus acceleration on a sinusoidal yaw sweep after subtracting a force sensor carried only by the direct-drive limb.
- Feedforward decoupling cuts aligned foot-tracking error from 5.7 mm to 2.5 mm. Joint sweeps track the target foot path to 4.78 mm (2.08% of limb length), with 0.91 deg mean roll error.
- Supporting at least 4.3 kg, peak motor torque drops from 10.3 to 3.4 N·m at the hip pitch (67.0%) and from 4.7 to 3.6 N·m at the knee (23.4%).

## Limitations
Roll tracking under load is worse than direct drive (5.50 vs 2.02 deg max error), which the authors attribute to elastic deformation along the longer cable path, and the cable routing makes maintenance harder. A multi-turn control issue limited motor-driven tests to part of the predicted range of motion; the folded swimming posture was only reached by hand. Integrating the limb into a complete robot for locomotion experiments is left to future work.
