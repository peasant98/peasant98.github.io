tldr: An open-source muscle module for tendon-driven robots built around a quasi-direct-drive motor with a 7.75:1 gear, so muscle tension can be set from motor current without a tension sensor. Compared with an earlier 53:1 module it needs about half the force to backdrive (about 4 N vs 8 N) and winds wire about 1.5x faster at comparable tension.
abstract: Tendon-driven robots, which actuate like biological muscles by using motors to wind wires, offer advantages such as variable stiffness and redundant actuation. Despite these advantages, musculoskeletal structures inherently involve a large number of diverse muscles, making it desirable to compactly modularize each muscle unit and flexibly accommodate diverse muscle arrangements. While various small muscle modules have been proposed to date, existing designs tend to be complex, difficult to maintain, and suffer from low backdrivability and limited actuation speed due to their high gear ratios. In this work, we propose QDD-MM, a muscle module that leverages recent advances in quasi-direct-drive motors to achieve a simpler and more compact structure with higher backdrivability and faster actuation than previous modules. By utilizing quasi-direct-drive motors, the module eliminates the need for dedicated force-sensing mechanisms, significantly simplifying the overall design, while also supporting flexible wire routing in multiple directions, various module-to-module connections, and even the use of the module body itself as a structural component. QDD-MM integrates these capabilities into a reusable muscle module, and its design is openly available at https://github.com/haraduka/qddmm. We developed tendon-driven robots with one joint and either two or three muscles, as well as a partial musculoskeletal humanoid, using the proposed QDD-MM and verified their effectiveness. We expect that this muscle module will democratize tendon-driven robotics and further accelerate progress in the field.

## What they do
Tendon-driven robots need many muscles, so each muscle should be a compact, reusable module. Earlier muscle modules pair small BLDC motors with high-ratio gearboxes (84:1, 53:1). That makes them hard to backdrive and slow, and it forces a separate tension sensor because current no longer tracks force. QDD-MM swaps in a commercial quasi-direct-drive motor with a 7.75:1 gear and drops the sensor. The design is open source.

## How it works
- **Main body.** A RobStride05 motor with a built-in driver on CAN winds 2 mm Zylon wire onto a 24 mm pulley. That gives 133 N of continuous tension, or 267 N with a 12 mm pulley. The module measures 46 x 58 x 82 mm and weighs 0.34 kgf.
- **Wire anchoring.** The wire weaves through seven small pulleys and is held by friction instead of a knot, so at 133 N only about 0.18 N reaches the wire end. Minimum bend radius goes from 0.1 mm to 3 mm.
- **Reconfigurable.** The routing unit mounts in several orientations, so the wire can leave forward, backward, left, right or up. Connectors join modules in parallel, or in series so the modules themselves act as bone.

## Results
- Backdriving at 5 mm/s takes about 4 N, against about 8 N for the previous module.
- Both modules deliver about 90% of the commanded tension. When the command drops from 200 N to 70 N, the previous module still pulls about 160 N, while QDD-MM pulls about 80 N.
- No-load winding speed is 603 mm/s (302 mm/s with the 12 mm pulley), against 200-216 mm/s for earlier modules.
- One-joint robots with two or three modules track a 0 to -90 degree command. Adding a third muscle roughly halves the tension on each agonist. Eight modules drive the scapula and upper arm of a shoulder together.

## Limitations
Cogging torque makes the backdriving force fluctuate more. Friction and hysteresis still add error to current-based tension estimates. The force and speed come from the commercial actuator and pulley size, not the module design. Durability and long-term maintenance are untested, and the shoulder's motors sit outside the body.
