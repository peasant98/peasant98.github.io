abstract: 'Robust visual servoing during robotic grasping remains challenging, as approach-phase occlusion frequently disrupts closed-loop control. To address this, we present an active observation framework in which one arm of an existing dual-arm platform carries an RGB-D camera as an active observer while the other executes grasping. The observer repositions to maintain or recover a shared target-gripper view within the feasible shared-view workspace. Occlusion during the final approach is particularly acute: fixed eye-to-hand lacks an active recovery mechanism once the target is hidden, while eye-in-hand can suffer from self-occlusion and sensitivity to hand-eye calibration. The observer first uses an active two-phase viewpoint strategy to align high-precision RGB-D axes with task-relevant error components. If visibility later degrades, an occlusion-triggered exploration module pauses the worker arm and scores candidate viewpoints by observation quality to recover a shared target-gripper view. Because both objects are detected in a single RGB-D image, the resulting Relative Servo Error avoids differencing two independently transformed target and gripper estimates. We evaluate the system on a dual 6-DoF Piper platform using an open-vocabulary detector with text prompts, without task-specific fine-tuning or demonstrations. Across 50 randomized trials per object, the system achieves 94% grasp success versus 76% for a reactive viewpoint baseline and 64% for a fixed camera. On triggered severe-occlusion trials, recovery takes $1.8 \pm 0.6$ s on average.'

## What they do
Visual servoing breaks when the arm hides the target during the final approach: a fixed camera cannot recover the view, and a wrist camera suffers self-occlusion. Here one arm of a dual-arm platform carries an RGB-D camera as an active observer while the other grasps. An open-vocabulary detector finds the target and the gripper tip from text prompts, so no demonstrations or training are needed.

## How it works
- **Relative Servo Error.** Target and gripper tip are detected in the same RGB-D image and their displacement is taken in the camera frame, before mapping to the robot base. This cancels translational hand-eye calibration error; rotational error leaves a bias that shrinks as the gripper nears the target.
- **Two-phase viewpoint.** Depth noise exceeds lateral noise, so the observer looks from the side and then the front at 0.23 m, putting the key error components on the low-noise lateral axes.
- **Occlusion recovery.** When detection confidence stays low, the worker pauses and the observer scores candidate viewpoints by confidence, box size, valid depth and stability.
- **Gated grasping.** A 20 Hz proportional controller moves only when both detections are confident, through approach, fine alignment and a blind final grasp, with a collision monitor between the arms.

## Results
- Two 6-DoF Piper arms and a RealSense D435i, nine objects, 50 randomized trials each: 94% success and 6.6 mm closure error, versus 76% / 10.4 mm (reactive observer), 70% (static observer camera), 64% / 12.7 mm (fixed camera) and 52% / 18.3 mm (wrist camera).
- Small objects (hex bolt, bottle cap, pen cap) reach 88-92%, versus 44-54% for the fixed camera. Recovery from severe occlusion takes 1.8 ± 0.6 s, versus 4.5 s for the reactive baseline.
- Ablations: without exploration success falls to 72%; without the relative error, closure error rises to 14.5 mm. With a 30 mm hand-eye offset injected, the full system keeps 86% success against 30% without the relative error.

## Limitations
Assumes stationary tabletop targets, top-down grasps and two arm bases on one plane with near-parallel orientation. Failures come from the worker arm outpacing the observer, joint limits blocking a good viewpoint, and depth holes from specular metal objects.
