abstract: Robotic simulators that represent shear within tactile sensing are becoming increasingly important for contact-rich manipulation. However, simulating contact shear with high-fidelity deformable or soft-body simulation is computationally demanding, limiting scalability to large-scale reinforcement learning. Here we introduce \textit{TactileLab}, a scalable multimodal tactile simulation framework built on IsaacLab, with a particular focus on a new optical-flow-based tactile representation termed \textit{simulated tactile flow}. Instead of relying on soft-body physics to model shear deformation, TactileLab uses rigid-body simulation to efficiently estimate dense tangential motion at the contact interface, producing tactile flow fields that encode shear-relevant contact dynamics. This flow field complements geometric features from a contact depth field. We further introduce a novel policy architecture for jointly processing long-horizon proprioceptive signals and rich tactile observations. Across multiple contact-rich tasks, policies trained using simulated tactile flow learn efficiently at scale and exhibit emergent contact-aware behaviours. To facilitate sim-to-real policy deployment, we then propose a novel real-to-sim tactile translation method that maps real tactile image streams to simulated tactile flow and depth fields, together with a new tactile data collection method, and validate it across diverse tactile sensor designs. Using this pipeline, we demonstrate real-world deployment on tasks encompassing dual-arm and dexterous in-hand manipulation. Overall, TactileLab provides an end-to-end sim-to-real framework for scalable tactile policy learning, enabling diverse shear-sensitive manipulation across heterogeneous tactile robotic systems.

## What they do
Rigid-body tactile simulators are fast but give only contact depth, which misses shear. Soft-body simulators capture shear but are too slow for large-scale RL. TactileLab, built on IsaacLab, adds *simulated tactile flow*: a dense 2D field of how each contact point moves tangentially across the sensor, computed from rigid-body contact kinematics. Policies train on depth and flow in simulation and run on real robots by translating real tactile images into the same representation.

## How it works
- **Tactile flow.** Contact-patch points are projected onto the image plane of a camera modelled inside the sensor. Their image-space displacement over one timestep of sensor-object relative motion is the flow, zero outside contact. No object SDF or elastomer model is needed.
- **Policy learning.** A teacher trained with RL on privileged state (object pose, mass, friction, perturbations) is distilled into a student. Its adaptation module, PETS-Net, combines positional-encoded proprioception-action histories with CNN-encoded depth and flow histories through a TCN.
- **Real-to-sim.** TacFlowNet, a pix2pix-style conditional GAN, maps two consecutive real tactile images to simulated flow. It is trained on paired real and simulated pressing, sliding, tilting and twisting motions.

## Results
- Simulated in-hand rotation under perturbations: depth plus flow has the lowest fall rate, 11.76% in distribution and 18.69% on unseen objects, against 47.66% and 57.85% for proprioception alone.
- Shear estimation from six real sensors: for a markerless GelSight Mini, translating to simulated flow cuts translational error from 1.79 to 0.34 mm/s. DIGIT stays weakest (3.33 °/s rotational error).
- Real dual-arm following, trained on a rigid cube moving in straight lines: 0.91 mm / 0.019 rad error on that cube, up to 6.43 mm / 0.057 rad on a foam brain moving in circles.
- Real LEAP Hand with four tactile fingertips, trained only on capsules: 1.95 rotations in 30 s with depth plus flow, against 1.70 depth only, 1.61 low-dimensional contact and 0.82 proprioception. On cube-shaped objects: 1.33 against 0.28 for low-dimensional contact.

## Limitations
Real-to-sim transfer assumes sticking contact; disturbances that cause slip can lead to unrecoverable states, and slip is not simulated. Handling more diverse object shapes would need larger real-to-sim datasets and geometry-aware transfer models.
