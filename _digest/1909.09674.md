abstract: 'Assistive robotic arms enable users with physical disabilities to perform everyday tasks without relying on a caregiver. Unfortunately, the very dexterity that makes these arms useful also makes them challenging to teleoperate: the robot has more degrees-of-freedom than the human can directly coordinate with a handheld joystick. Our insight is that we can make assistive robots easier for humans to control by leveraging latent actions. Latent actions provide a low-dimensional embedding of high-dimensional robot behavior: for example, one latent dimension might guide the assistive arm along a pouring motion. In this paper, we design a teleoperation algorithm for assistive robots that learns latent actions from task demonstrations. We formulate the controllability, consistency, and scaling properties that user-friendly latent actions should have, and evaluate how different low-dimensional embeddings capture these properties. Finally, we conduct two user studies on a robotic arm to compare our latent action approach to both state-of-the-art shared autonomy baselines and a teleoperation strategy currently used by assistive arms. Participants completed assistive eating and cooking tasks more efficiently when leveraging our latent actions, and also subjectively reported that latent actions made the task easier to perform. The video accompanying this paper can be found at: https://youtu.be/wjnhrzugBj4.'

## What they do
Assistive arms have more degrees of freedom than a user can coordinate with a joystick. The paper learns *latent actions*: a low-DoF embedding of demonstrated robot actions, so that a 1- or 2-DoF joystick input is decoded into a full high-DoF action. It defines what makes latent actions user-friendly (controllability, consistency, scaling), tests which autoencoders provide them in simulation, and runs two user studies on a 7-DoF Fetch arm.

## How it works
- **Autoencoder.** An encoder maps a demonstrated state-action pair to a latent z, and a decoder reconstructs the action. The loss is reconstruction error, plus a KL term towards a unit normal for the VAE variants.
- **State conditioning.** The decoder takes both z and the current state (cAE, cVAE), so the same joystick push can mean "pour" when the robot holds a cup. Baselines are PCA, AE and VAE without state input.
- **Control.** The learned latent axes are rotated by hand to line up with the joystick axes. At run time the joystick input is z and the decoder outputs the robot action.

## Results
- Simulation (planar 5-joint arms): on Sine, cAE and cVAE reach 1.37% and 3.74% of PCA's reconstruction loss, versus 98.0% and 100% for AE and VAE; on Rotate, 0.65% and 0.84%. On Circle the two latent directions are 72 and 74 degrees apart for cAE and cVAE, versus 27 and 34 for AE and VAE (90 is ideal).
- Eating (10 users): with cVAE, users reached their intended morsel in 44 of 50 trials, a higher success rate than the shared-autonomy baselines. Completion time, joystick input and trajectory length were all significantly lower than with High Assist.
- Cooking (11 users, within-subjects; cVAE trained on under 7 minutes of kinesthetic demonstrations): completion time and joystick input dropped compared with end-effector teleoperation (p < .001), and users rated cVAE as less effort, easier and more natural.

## Limitations
Outside the states covered by demonstrations the latent actions became erratic: one participant turned the arm upside-down while pouring flour and could not guide it back. Users did not clearly prefer latent actions, saying they enjoyed the freedom of end-effector control. In the eating study participants used only cVAE; the baselines come from the HARMONIC dataset.
