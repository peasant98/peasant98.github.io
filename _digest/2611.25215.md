abstract: Autonomous navigation in diverse real-world environments remains one of the greatest challenges in robotics. It demands both robust low-level motion control to handle diverse terrain and high-level reasoning to interpret environmental cues. We present ROAM (Robust Open-world Autonomous Mobility), a system that enables legged robots to perform kilometer-scale navigation in complex alpine and urban settings. ROAM combines the world knowledge of a Vision-Language Model (VLM), pre-trained on internet-scale data, with robust locomotion and local planning policies learned in simulation via reinforcement learning (RL). The VLM provides common-sense reasoning and semantic interpretation of the scene, assessing risks, interpreting signs, and producing semantic labels with associated navigation costs. An open-vocabulary segmentation module converts these labels into a dense, real-time risk map, which conditions two pre-trained RL policies responsible for agile locomotion and local navigation, effectively coupling high-level VLM reasoning with low-level physical control. Real-world experiments showed the system's ability to autonomously navigate a wide range of complex real-world environments. The robot completes kilometer-long hikes with minimal intervention, interprets signs to avoid danger, and evaluates its environment to identify safe areas on which to step. This work represents a step toward the reliable deployment of systems in unstructured environments. However, it also underscores the challenges that remain in VLM reliability and spatial reasoning, indicating that foundational models are a crucial component but not yet a complete solution for navigation in the wild.

## What they do
Geometric traversability misses hazards like water or tall grass and ignores norms like staying on the trail. ROAM gives a legged robot this common sense without asking a VLM for coordinates or motor commands. The VLM only names what it sees and scores it; segmentation, mapping and RL policies handle the spatial part. The system runs on an ANYmal D in alpine, urban and indoor settings.

## How it works
- **Scene evaluation.** Every 10 s, GPT-4 Vision gets the front and rear camera images plus a prompt describing the robot. It returns labels with risk, steppable and preferred-area values (0 to 1), plus a memory note fed into later queries. A YOLO detector pauses the robot when it sees a sign so the VLM can read it.
- **Maps.** Open-vocabulary segmentation runs at 6 Hz and turns the labels into per-pixel value images. These are fused into a 4 cm, 8 x 8 m elevation map as extra layers.
- **Policies.** A teacher-student locomotion policy takes height and steppable scans and is penalized for non-steppable footholds. An RL local navigation policy takes risk and elevation layers, and non-preferred areas get a virtual height increase. Both are trained in simulation on these abstract inputs; the global route comes from hiking-path data.

## Results
- Zermatt: 10.1 km with a 1220 m descent at 96.7% autonomy in time, with two interventions at construction zones. Seealpsee: 2.38 km at 97.5%.
- Mannequin on the path, 10 trials: ROAM reached the goal every time with no collisions. The geometry-only baseline hit it every time; ViNT reached the goal 60% of the time and collided in 90%.
- Path generation on 27 hand-labeled images: 99.1% of ROAM trajectory pixels lay in safe regions and 86.8% in preferred ones. Asking the VLM for pixel coordinates gave at most 71.4% and 39.8%.

## Limitations
Segmentation sometimes fails to match the VLM's labels: it missed a fence the VLM had named. The system needs a global reference path, depends on a cloud VLM and network, and was tested mainly in daylight. Field runs were done once per method.
