abstract: 'Pretrained robot policies decide what to do from camera images, yet some of the physics that decides whether their plans succeed is not visible. An object may look light but be 100 times heavier, and a weakened joint or a lagging controller leaves no trace in the image. When the physics at deployment differs from training, the plan is often still right but is executed as if nothing had changed. Training on such variations in advance cannot handle unseen changes, while adapting once deployed either changes the plan, leaving the miscalibrated commands in place, or updates a robot model by gradient steps that do not know how reliable they are and can make the robot worse even when nothing has changed. We propose RoboAda, which keeps the policy and its plan unchanged and corrects the commands that carry it out by inferring at test time how the robot turns commands into motion. The key observation is that the robot''s own motion reveals the change: after each action chunk, an inverse-dynamics model of the original robot converts the observed motion into the commands it would have needed, yielding free labels at every step. Competing hypotheses of how the robot responds, including that nothing has changed, are weighted by how well they predict these labels, and the next chunk is sent with the commands that best realize the plan under them. RoboAda needs no reward, no gradient step and no data from the changed robot. We evaluate RoboAda with five frozen policies on LIBERO, two world action models and three vision-language-action models. Under eleven physical shifts, it more than doubles their average success, from 27.8% to 62.9%. It beats the best of seven test-time adaptation methods by 17.3 points on Cosmos Policy, and largely preserves the policies'' success when nothing has changed. On bimanual RoboTwin 2.0 in joint space, it raises success under joint-target offsets from 24.9% to 78.8%.'

## What they do
Pretrained manipulation policies fail when the physics changes in ways the camera cannot see: a weaker actuator, a lagging controller, a payload, or an object 100 times heavier than it looks. RoboAda argues that the plan is usually still right and only the commands that carry it out are wrong. It leaves the policy untouched and corrects those commands at test time, with no reward, no gradient step and no data from the changed robot.

## How it works
- **Hindsight labels.** After each action chunk, an inverse-dynamics model trained on the nominal robot turns the observed proprioception into the command the nominal robot would have needed. If the robot executes half of every command, the label is half the command sent.
- **Body hypotheses.** Per action axis, three hypotheses are fit to these labels by recursive least squares: unchanged, affine (gain and offset), and state-dependent (for example a load that appears at the grasp). Each is weighted by how well it predicted labels it had not yet seen, so execution noise does not pull weight away from the unchanged body.
- **Acting.** The next chunk's commands minimize the expected tracking error to the plan under the weighted hypotheses, so corrections shrink where the fit is uncertain.
- Features and priors are meta-learned once on nominal Cosmos Policy rollouts with synthetic body changes, and reused unchanged for all policies.

## Results
- LIBERO, five frozen policies (Cosmos Policy, Fast-WAM, π0, π0.5, OpenVLA-OFT), eleven physical shifts: average success rises from 27.8% to 62.9%. Without a shift it stays within 4.0 points of the frozen policy.
- On Cosmos Policy it leads the best of seven re-implemented test-time adaptation methods, GrBAL, by 17.3 points (69.6% vs 52.3%).
- With objects 100 times heavier, Cosmos Policy rises from 58.0% to 76.5%. The fitted gain reaches the true value within four to six chunks.
- On bimanual RoboTwin 2.0 with offset joint targets, Fast-WAM rises from 24.9% to 78.8%.

## Limitations
All experiments are in simulation. RoboAda corrects only how a plan is executed, not visual, language or scene shifts, and only effects that a transformation of the commands can express. Couplings between axes enter only through the learned features, and there is no closed-loop stability guarantee.
