## What they do
Locomotion policies trained in simulation handle environments like their training set, but when they fail out of distribution the usual fix is to redesign rewards or environments and retrain. Walk These Ways instead trains one quadruped policy, on flat ground only, that can solve the same velocity-tracking task in many different ways (Multiplicity of Behavior, MoB). A human operator then picks the behavior in real time for the situation at hand.

## How it works
- **Behavior parameters.** Besides the velocity command, the policy takes an 8-D behavior vector: timing offsets between feet (pronking, trotting, bounding, pacing and their interpolations), stepping frequency, body height, body pitch, stance width and footswing height.
- **Rewards.** Velocity-tracking task rewards, fixed auxiliary rewards for stability and smoothness, and augmented auxiliary rewards for matching the behavior vector. Stance width is tracked through the Raibert heuristic so it does not conflict with fast turning, and the total reward r_task·exp(0.02·r_aux) always pays for task progress.
- **Training and deployment.** PPO in Isaac Gym, with command and behavior resampled within each episode so the policy learns transitions. An MLP sees a 30-step history of joint states, gravity vector, commands and actions. Domain randomization, an actuator network and a modeled 20 ms delay support zero-shot transfer to a Unitree Go1 running at 50 Hz.

## Results
- Real robot: all gait parameters are tracked after transfer. A high footswing climbs curbs and stairs without tripping, a lowered body crawls under a 22 cm bar (9 cm clearance), a wide stance braces against shoves, and switching from a fast trot to pronking produces a 60 cm leap.
- Simulated platform terrain (up to 16 cm, never seen in training): survival fraction 0.88 for trotting and 0.87 for pacing vs 0.83 for the gait-free baseline.
- Simulated power consumption, not a training objective: trotting uses 24 vs the baseline's 35 at 1 m/s.

## Limitations
MoB costs in-distribution performance: on flat ground the gait-free baseline tracks velocity better (xy reward 0.94 vs 0.92 for trotting), mostly for combined high linear and angular velocity. A human pilot must choose the behavior; automatic selection is left to future work. The real-world terrain results are demonstrations without reported success rates.
