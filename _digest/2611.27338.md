abstract: Given a pretrained general latent-conditioned whole-body humanoid controller, say a behavioral foundation model (BFM), how can we elicit complex and contact-rich out-of-distribution behaviors? Raw joint-action exploration enables direct, task-specific adjustments but can disrupt coordination learned by the pretrained controller, whereas latent exploration retains that coordination but is difficult to steer toward desired physical effects. To combine the complementary strengths of these exploration spaces, we propose Latent Jacobian Steer (LJS), which couples sampling in direct joint-action space with the semantics of the learned frozen controller's input latent space. For a task specified through desired physical effects, we sample task-relevant joint-action perturbations and map them through a damped local inverse of the policy Jacobian to obtain candidate latent ($z$) commands. These commands are executed through the frozen pretrained policy, while exploration is directed toward the physical outcomes required by the new task. Candidate outcomes are evaluated in simulation and composed into latent-command sequences that accomplish the task. Ablations show that neither action-space nor latent-space exploration alone provides the same capability as their coupling. Across diverse out-of-distribution tasks, simulation-based searching using this method produces contact-rich behaviors, including hurdling, manipulating articulated objects, and opening doors through non-prehensile contact. Over ten seeds, LJS completes more of each humanoid task than when searching in either space alone. Beyond humanoids, it also steers a goal-conditioned dexterous hand policy through four assembly tasks better than searching in goal space directly.

## What they do
Behavioral foundation models such as BFM-Zero are pretrained whole-body humanoid controllers that take a latent command, but they were never trained on object or environment interaction. Latent Jacobian Steer (LJS) keeps such a controller frozen and searches for latent-command sequences that make it vault a hurdle, lift a sliding shutter and walk under it, or open a door while carrying a cube. It uses no demonstrations, reference motions or retraining. All experiments run in simulation, mainly MuJoCo with the 29-DoF Unitree G1.

## How it works
- **Tasks as physical effects.** Each task is an ordered list of measurable outcomes, such as "palms under the shutter", then "shutter lifted", then "torso through". Each outcome has a score and an acceptance test. An LLM coding assistant turned the descriptions into simulator measurements, which the authors reviewed. Finalizing one task took under five hours.
- **Jacobian pull-back.** CEM samples changes to the 29 joint targets. A damped least-squares inverse of the policy's Jacobian with respect to its 256-D latent maps each change into a latent update, so every executed action still comes from the pretrained controller.
- **Search loop.** Each decision simulates 1,024 candidates for 40 control steps from a saved state and executes the best one for 20 steps. The search moves to the next effect once the current one is accepted.

## Results
- Fully completed tasks over ten seeds: LJS completes door-cube 10/10 and the shutter 7/10. Direct latent search completes 6/10 and 0/10, and residual search on the joint targets completes 0/10 on both. On the hurdle, LJS and residual search each complete 3/10.
- Steering the frozen Play2Perfect hand policy through its goal input, LJS completes 31 of 40 assembly runs, against 21 for direct goal search.
- On the 65 effects that both LJS and residual search complete, LJS uses a median 1.59 W/kg of mechanical power, against 2.61, and less than half the joint jerk.
- With retries under randomized physics, LJS completes all 30 runs, against 22 for the best baseline. A tracking policy trained from one LJS table climb succeeds in 96% of randomized trials.

## Limitations
LJS is an offline procedure. It needs privileged simulator measurements, saved-state resets and many parallel rollouts, so it cannot run in real time. It can only reach behaviors the frozen controller supports, and the hand-written effects can admit unintended solutions. There are no real-robot experiments; the authors leave sim-to-real to future work.
