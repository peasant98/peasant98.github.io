## What they do
Gato applies the large-language-model recipe to control. Every modality (text, image patches, proprioception, joint torques, button presses) is serialized into one flat token sequence, and a single decoder-only transformer is trained on it by supervised next-token prediction. The same weights play Atari, navigate DM Lab, caption images, chat and stack blocks with a real Sawyer arm. Model size was held to about 1.2B parameters so it can control a real robot in real time.

## How it works
- **Tokenization.** SentencePiece text (32,000 subwords); 16x16 image patches embedded by a ResNet block; continuous values mu-law encoded and binned into 1024 levels. Each timestep is observation tokens, a separator, then action tokens.
- **Model and loss.** 1.18B-parameter transformer, 24 layers, 1024-token context. The loss covers only text and logged actions, not image or observation tokens.
- **Data.** Trajectories from specialist RL agents, filtered to episodes with at least 80% of expert return, mixed with MassiveText and vision-language datasets. Trained for 1M steps on a 16x16 TPU v3 slice (about 4 days).
- **Task specification.** No task IDs: 25% of training sequences get a prompt from another episode of the same task, and at evaluation Gato is prompted with a successful demonstration.

## Results
- Above 50% of expert score on over 450 of 604 tasks; human level or better on 23 Atari games; over 50% on 44 of 45 Meta-World tasks.
- Real-robot RGB Stacking with unseen object shapes: 50.2% average success, against 49% for the single-task BC-IMP baseline. With fine-tuning it matches the expert after 10 episodes and exceeds it at 100-1000.
- A new real task, stacking blue on green: after fine-tuning on 500 demonstrations (2 h 45 min), 60% success, against 0.5% for BC trained from scratch.
- For the same number of training tokens, larger models score higher (79M < 364M < 1.18B).

## Limitations
Gato learns only by imitating RL-agent data, and no web-scale control dataset exists. With images, the 1024-token context holds only a few timesteps, so demonstration prompts did not significantly help on new environments; adapting needed fine-tuning. On Atari the single-task experts still beat it, pretraining gave no benefit on the held-out boxing game, and its dialogue and captioning are rudimentary.
