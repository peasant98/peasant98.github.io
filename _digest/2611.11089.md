abstract: Vision-based control policies, including vision-language-action models, have enabled robots to perform increasingly complex tasks. However, their execution speed is often limited by the high computational cost of action inference. In this work, we investigate what can be achieved at the control level without modifying the underlying policy learning process. We introduce an asynchronous inference and control framework that enables fast task execution while maintaining smooth and stable robot motions. By formulating the generation of continuous target positions from discontinuous model predictions as a tracking control problem, our method makes asynchronous inference practical for real robots. We further accelerate execution through adaptive action skipping based on the variance of predicted positions. Experiments with Action Chunking Transformer and OpenVLA-OFT on a bimanual robot demonstrate substantial speedups while preserving high task success rates.

## What they do
Vision-based policies such as ACT and OpenVLA are slow to execute because the robot waits for each action inference. Running inference asynchronously removes the wait, but new action chunks arrive late and do not line up with the old ones, so the commands jump. This paper fixes the problem at the control level, without retraining the policy: a command generator tracks the policy's outputs smoothly, and an adaptive rule skips actions when the predictions are reliable.

## How it works
- **Control-aware command generation (CAC).** Policy outputs are treated as desired joint positions. The command sent to the robot follows them with a feedforward term plus a saturated feedback term, analogous to sliding mode control, and its acceleration is clipped to fixed bounds.
- **Two threads.** A control thread sends commands and picks up a new action chunk whenever the inference thread has one; otherwise it keeps using the previous chunk. Observations and chunks carry time stamps.
- **Adaptive skipping.** Several chunks predict the action for the same step. If the variance of those predictions is below a threshold (0.2 in all experiments), the step is skipped and time advances by two steps.

## Results
- Real bimanual ALOHA, three tasks, 50 demonstrations each, 10 trials for each of three seeds.
- ACT with CAC, asynchronous inference and adaptive skipping takes 53-63% of the time of synchronous ACT: box handover 8.6 s vs 16.2 s, case stacking 19.2 s vs 34.4 s, pen storage 34.4 s vs 52.6 s.
- Pen storage success rises from 30.0% (synchronous ACT) to 83.3%; the other two tasks stay at 96.7-100%. Uniform skipping instead drops pen storage to 0%.
- With OpenVLA-OFT on pen storage, success stays at 8/10 while episode duration falls from 68.2 s to 59.0 s; without CAC, asynchronous OpenVLA-OFT oscillated and could not reliably complete the task.

## Limitations
There is no comparison with generic smoothing methods or with Real-Time Chunking, the inpainting approach for diffusion and flow-matching policies. OpenVLA-OFT was run with default parameters, including chunk size, on one task with 10 trials. Evaluation covers one robot and three tasks.
