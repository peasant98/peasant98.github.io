abstract: Mobility in an effective and socially-compliant manner is an essential yet challenging task for robots operating in crowded spaces. Recent works have shown the power of deep reinforcement learning techniques to learn socially cooperative policies. However, their cooperation ability deteriorates as the crowd grows since they typically relax the problem as a one-way Human-Robot interaction problem. In this work, we want to go beyond first-order Human-Robot interaction and more explicitly model Crowd-Robot Interaction (CRI). We propose to (i) rethink pairwise interactions with a self-attention mechanism, and (ii) jointly model Human-Robot as well as Human-Human interactions in the deep reinforcement learning framework. Our model captures the Human-Human interactions occurring in dense crowds that indirectly affects the robot's anticipation capability. Our proposed attentive pooling mechanism learns the collective importance of neighboring humans with respect to their future states. Various experiments demonstrate that our model can anticipate human dynamics and navigate in crowds with time efficiency, outperforming state-of-the-art methods.

## What they do
Earlier RL crowd-navigation policies aggregate the crowd crudely (CADRL keeps one worst-case human via maximin; LSTM-RL feeds humans in order of distance) and model only how humans affect the robot. This paper learns a value network that encodes every robot-human pair, includes a coarse model of human-human interaction, and weights each human with a learned self-attention score.

## How it works
- **Interaction module.** In a robot-centric frame, an MLP embeds each human's state, the robot's state and a 4x4 local map (1 m cells holding the presence and velocity of that human's neighbors), then a second MLP gives a pairwise interaction feature. The map avoids explicit O(N^2) human-human modeling.
- **Attentive pooling.** An MLP scores each human from its embedding plus the mean embedding of all humans. The weighted sum of interaction features gives a fixed-length crowd vector for any number of humans.
- **Planning.** An MLP maps the crowd vector to a state value; actions come from 80 discrete holonomic velocities (5 speeds x 16 headings).
- **Training.** Imitation warm start on 3k ORCA episodes, then 10k episodes of temporal-difference learning (about 10 h on a CPU) against ORCA-driven simulated humans.

## Results
- Robot invisible to humans (500 test cases): SARL and LM-SARL succeed 1.00, versus 0.95 for LSTM-RL, 0.78 for CADRL and 0.43 for ORCA. LM-SARL navigates in 10.46 s versus 11.82 s for LSTM-RL.
- Robot visible: LM-SARL succeeds 1.00 in 10.59 s (reward 0.334). ORCA succeeds 0.99 but takes 12.29 s (reward 0.284); CADRL collides in 0.03 of cases.
- The local map changes reward by under 0.01, since human-human interactions matter only some of the time; qualitatively, only LM-SARL's value map favors cutting behind humans 1 and 3 when human 3 might turn toward the robot.

## Limitations
Assumes a holonomic robot that reaches commanded velocities instantly, and fully observed human positions, velocities and radii. Training queries the simulator for the true next state; deployment would need a trajectory predictor. All numbers come from one simulated circle-crossing scenario with ORCA humans; the Segway robot test is shown only as a video.
