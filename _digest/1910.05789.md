abstract: While we would like agents that can coordinate with humans, current algorithms such as self-play and population-based training create agents that can coordinate with themselves. Agents that assume their partner to be optimal or similar to them can converge to coordination protocols that fail to understand and be understood by humans. To demonstrate this, we introduce a simple environment that requires challenging coordination, based on the popular game Overcooked, and learn a simple model that mimics human play. We evaluate the performance of agents trained via self-play and population-based training. These agents perform very well when paired with themselves, but when paired with our human model, they are significantly worse than agents designed to play with the human model. An experiment with a planning algorithm yields the same conclusion, though only when the human-aware planner is given the exact human model that it is playing with. A user study with real humans shows this pattern as well, though less strongly. Qualitatively, we find that the gains come from having the agent adapt to the human's gameplay. Given this result, we suggest several approaches for designing agents that learn about humans in order to better coordinate with them. Code is available at https://github.com/HumanCompatibleAI/overcooked_ai.

## What they do
Self-play works in competitive games because a human's suboptimal move only helps the agent. In a common-payoff game the same mistake hurts the team, and co-trained agents can settle on conventions humans don't share. The authors test this in a simplified two-chef Overcooked kitchen with five layouts designed to make motion or strategy coordination hard.

## How it works
- **Task.** Put 3 onions in a pot, wait 20 timesteps, plate and serve the soup for a shared reward of 20. Teams are scored over 400 timesteps.
- **Human models.** About 16 human-human games per layout (18k timesteps in total) are split into two halves. Behavior cloning on a hand-designed 64-D state featurization gives a training model (BC) and a held-out test partner (H_Proxy).
- **Agents trained with themselves.** PPO self-play (SP), population-based training (PBT) with 3 agents, and a joint planner that replans after every partner action.
- **Agents trained for humans.** PPO with BC embedded in the environment (PPO_BC), annealed from self-play, and an A* planner that queries the partner model. As a gold standard, both are also trained with H_Proxy itself.

## Results
- With H_Proxy, SP and PBT do very well with themselves but struggle to match the pure imitation agent BC. PPO_BC outperforms them and gets closer to the gold standard.
- On the 2 layouts where planning was feasible, the joint planner drops far below the gold standard with H_Proxy; much of the drop seems to come from expecting optimal motion. Planning with BC instead of the exact model gets stuck in loops.
- 60 Mechanical Turk users, between-subjects: PPO_BC beats SP and PBT in three layouts and roughly matches the better one in the other two. Agent type has a significant effect (F(2,224)=6.49, p<.01). With non-cooperative players kept in, PPO_BC vs SP falls to p=.06.
- SP and PBT predict real human actions poorly, while BC and H_Proxy do decently.

## Limitations
The human model is plain, non-adaptive behavior cloning; it is very poor on Forced Coordination and Counter Circuit, which the authors suspect is why PPO_BC helps less there. They guess real users adapt to the agent, which the stationary model cannot capture. Each agent is trained for one layout, and the authors cannot fully rule out that BC helps only by exposing the agent to more varied states.
