abstract: Reinforcement learning policies for robot manipulation degrade when the number of objects N in the scene grows beyond the training range. This is a covariate shift that also changes the dimension of the state. We propose WAVE (Warping Actions and Views of an Expert), which extends a pretrained single-object skill to many objects without updating its weights. WAVE has three parts. First, a selection policy picks the one object the robot works on next. Second, a warping policy reads a local view of the scene around that object, whose size does not depend on N. It emits a multiplier and an offset for every quantity the skill reads and every action it outputs. Third, the skill acts under this affine warp. It is shown a scene that holds a single object, while the warp steers it around the objects it is not shown and edits the actions that are sent to the robot. Both the selection and warping policies are trained with PPO on the multi-object reward and use the skill as a black box, so WAVE is task-general and skill-agnostic. It requires only that the skill was trained on the same embodiment and that every object has a goal pose. We also bound the number of attempts needed to place all N objects, assuming positive expected net progress per attempt, and measure this progress for each object count. We evaluate WAVE on cube arrangement tasks with a gantry robot and with a Franka Panda arm. Trained with at most nine cubes, it generalizes zero-shot to several times its training count, on the gantry to scenes of up to thirty cubes. Published methods that learn multi-object manipulation show count generalization up to six objects. Fine-tuning the skill, learning from the same local view without it, and an entity transformer, even when distilled from the pretrained skill, all collapse within their training counts under the same training budget.

## What they do
RL manipulation policies trained with a few objects break down when deployed with many. WAVE keeps a pretrained single-object skill frozen and learns two policies around it: one picks the next cube, the other edits what the skill sees and commands, steering it around cubes it is not shown.

## How it works
- **Frozen skill.** A TD7 policy that moves one cube to any goal pose (0.97 success alone), shown only the selected cube and its goal.
- **Warping policy.** A small transformer reads a fixed-width crop around the selected cube, so its input does not grow with the count. Every step it emits a multiplier and offset for each of the skill's 55 input and output channels; a zero multiplier takes a channel over.
- **Selection policy.** Scores unplaced cubes by learned priority, feasibility and travel; orderings such as stacking bottom-up are learned.
- Both train with PPO in MuJoCo on a reward that only counts cubes at their goals, with at most six to nine cubes per scene.

## Results
- Gantry stacking, trained with up to seven cubes: at least 0.92 whole-task success up to twenty cubes, 0.48 at thirty (line 0.90, pyramid 0.08). Published methods report at most 0.75 at six objects, on other tasks and simulators.
- Nine of ten baselines, including skill fine-tuning and an entity transformer, reach at most 0.02 at six cubes. A per-episode input edit gets 0.60 at ten cubes and 0.08 at twenty, against 0.98 and 0.95 for WAVE.
- Simulated Panda: pusher line 0.95 at nine cubes, 0.62 at twenty; gripper stacking 0.75 at six, 0.37 at ten.
- Real Panda, two towers from five cubes: all cubes placed in 8 of 10 runs, released with both towers standing in 5 of 10.

## Limitations
Every result except the physical Panda demonstration is simulated, and Panda gripper tasks degrade within their training range. Every object needs a goal pose, and the main experiments use identical cubes. The policy never learned the final release, and at high counts episodes mostly time out re-placing knocked-off cubes.
