tldr: A wearable robotic tail swings a 3 kg counterweight behind the user to offset a load held out in front. In a 10-person shelf-loading study it cut peak centre-of-pressure displacement by 17-20 mm and peak trunk inclination by 4.3 degrees compared with no tail.
abstract: 'Frequent heavy lifting is linked to musculoskeletal strain, with lower back pain affecting up to 40% of manual labourers. Inspired by animals using tails for balance, we developed a wearable robotic tail as a dynamic counterbalance. Ten participants performed an imitated shelf loading/unloading task across three conditions: active tail, passive tail, and no tail. Kinematics, centre of pressure, and muscle activation (gastrocnemius, erector spinae, tibialis anterior, biceps brachii) were measured. Compared with no tail, the active tail achieved >20 mm reduction in centre of pressure peak displacement, 4.3 degree reduction in trunk motion, and >9% and >20% reductions in erector spinae and gastrocnemius activity, respectively. The passive tail produced smaller reductions, suggesting passive counterbalancing helps while active movement augments it further. Biceps brachii activation increased with the active versus passive tail. The ankle kinematics and tibialis anterior activation were unchanged. These findings support wearable robotic tails for improving balance during material handling.'

## What they do
Placing a load on a shelf in front of you pushes your centre of pressure (CoP) toward your toes and makes you lean forward. Instead of an exoskeleton, the authors test a supernumerary limb: a wearable tail that swings backwards to counterbalance the load. Ten participants aged 20-31 moved a 4 kg load onto and off a 135 cm platform 40 cm in front of their feet, with the tail active, worn but unpowered (passive), and not worn.

## How it works
- **Hardware.** The tail, from the authors' earlier work, is a 3 kg counterweight that can extend 50 cm behind the user; the whole system weighs about 6 kg.
- **Load sensing.** The load sits on a handheld platform with force sensors, tethered to the wearable frame by a pull-rope encoder and a rotary encoder that measure its distance from the body.
- **Control.** A static model gives the CoP offset caused by the load; the tail angle that produces an equal and opposite offset is tracked by a PD controller on a Teensy 4.1, with under 60 ms latency.
- **Measurements.** Force plate, motion capture of trunk and ankle, and EMG on erector spinae, gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior and biceps; 15 repetitions per condition in random order.

## Results
- Peak CoP displacement, active tail vs no tail: 17 mm (22%) lower in placement, 20 mm (22%) lower in retrieval. The passive tail did not differ from no tail.
- Peak trunk inclination fell 4.3 degrees (47%) with the active tail and 2.4 degrees with the passive tail; ankle angles did not change.
- Average erector spinae activity, active vs no tail: 9% lower in placement, 28% lower in retrieval. Peak gastrocnemius activity fell 36% in placement.
- Integrated biceps activity rose 15% with the active vs passive tail during retrieval.

## Limitations
An imitated shelving task in the lab, with ten non-professional participants and one 4 kg load. The prototype still needs to become more compact, portable and power-efficient. Movement duration was not measured, so the biceps increase is not explained.
